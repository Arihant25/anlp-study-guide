When does counting help you choose?

Our project is titled Predicting When Collection Statistics Help Top-k Selection: From Retrieval to Attention and Expert Routing. This page explains the mid report in plain words: the idea, what we built, what ran, what the numbers say, and the part that did not work.

mid · 30 Sep 2026 182 retrieval rows 7 bases · 13 collections BM25 · Quest · OLMoE

Aviral Gupta · Mohit Kumar Singh · Anirudh Sankar · Arihant Tripathy — IIIT Hyderabad

§0The short version

Search engines, the attention inside a language model, and mixture-of-experts routing all do the same two steps. They score an input against a set of features with a dot product, then keep the top k. Classical search learnt decades ago to add a correction to the first step, called BM25. The two newer mechanisms don't use it. We asked when that correction helps, and whether you can predict it before running the experiment.

  1. Idea. BM25 adds three things to a plain dot product: IDF (rare features count more), saturation (repeating a feature has diminishing returns) and length normalisation (long documents don't win just by being long). We made each one an on/off switch, which gives 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 scorers.
  2. What we built. Fourteen sparse "concept" representations, all derived from one frozen encoder (multilingual E5). We scored each on 13 English and Indic retrieval collections under all 8 scorers: 182 rows × 8 configurations. The same scorer is also plugged into KV-cache page selection (Llama-3.2-1B) and expert routing (OLMoE). Those two are built and tested but have not run at scale yet.
  3. Result 1. The full BM25 correction helps in 161 of 179 rows and never costs more than 0.01 nDCG@10. The biggest gains are about 0.5.
  4. Result 2. Most of the gain comes from saturation (+7.8 points) and length normalisation (+3.7). IDF, the collection statistic we set out to study, adds the least (+1.5), and it is the only part that ever hurts.
  5. Result 3. Some margins can be worked out on paper. They come out exactly as predicted (±2) on every collection, which checks the whole pipeline end to end.
  6. Result 4 (the failure). The predictor we pre-registered does no better than always guessing "the correction helps". The cause is the way we standardised the target: it saturates near 3 for almost every row. We report that honestly and change the plan around it.

§1Background you need

§1.1 · Retrieval as a dot product

A retriever takes a query and a large pile of documents (the collection or corpus) and returns the documents most likely to answer it, in order. Most modern retrievers turn the query into a vector uq and every document into a vector ud, score each document with the dot product ⟨uq, ud⟩, and keep the top k.

A dense retriever such as DPR or E5 uses vectors where all 768 numbers are active and can be negative. A sparse retriever uses long vectors, one slot per "feature", where almost every slot is zero and the non-zero ones are positive. Old-school keyword search is sparse: one slot per word. Learned sparse retrieval (SPLADE, SAE retrievers) lets a model decide what the features are. That is the family this project lives in.

§1.2 · What BM25 adds to the dot product

BM25 is the classical keyword scoring function, and it is still a strong baseline. Compared with a plain dot product it makes three corrections. Each one fixes a specific way raw counts mislead.

ComponentThe problem it fixesWhat it does
IDF
inverse document frequency		 A word like "the" appears in every document, so matching it tells you nothing. Multiplies each feature by a weight that is large when few documents contain it and near zero when all of them do.
Saturation A document that says "cat" 50 times is not 50× more about cats than one that says it once. Squashes each feature's value through a curve that rises fast and then flattens, capped at k1+1.
Length normalisation Long documents match more things just by being long. Divides by how long the document is compared with the average.
numbers to make it concrete

IDF (with N = 1,000,000 documents): a feature in 10 documents gets weight ≈ 11.5, one in half the documents gets ln 2 ≈ 0.69, and one in every document gets ≈ 0.0000005.

Saturation with k1 = 1.2 and a document of average length: a raw value of 1 stays 1.00, 5 becomes 1.77, 50 becomes only 2.15. Nothing can go above 2.2.

BM25 needs three things from a representation: non-negative values (so saturation makes sense), a meaningful count of how many documents each feature appears in (for IDF), and a notion of document length. A dense embedding has none of them, because every coordinate is active everywhere and can be negative. That is why dense retrievers stick to the dot product. A sparse representation has all three.

§1.3 · Concept vocabularies from a dense encoder

You can turn a dense embedding into sparse features in several ways: cluster the embeddings and use the cluster IDs as features, quantise them into codes, or train a sparse autoencoder (SAE), a small network that rewrites each vector as a few active features out of thousands. Recent papers do exactly this and then use the features as a vocabulary for retrieval. §5 lists the seven ways we used.

§2The observation: three things that are one thing

The whole project rests on one observation. Three mechanisms from three different research communities compute the same thing: a non-negative score per feature, then a hard top-k.

SettingThe "query"The "documents"What is kept
A · Learned sparse retrievalquery vector uq document vectors udtop-k documents
B · KV-page selection (Quest)current attention query qt summaries of pages of 16 cached keystop pages to attend to
C · MoE routingtoken hidden state ht expert embeddings wetop-k experts

None of the three uses how often a feature gets selected across the whole collection, which is the statistic IDF is built on. Some fragments of the idea show up inside transformers under other names:

  • Loss-free balancing (an MoE training trick) adds a per-expert bias that goes down when an expert is used too often. That is a frequency discount, applied during training.
  • StreamingLLM always keeps the first few tokens in the KV cache, because they soak up lots of attention (they are "attention sinks").
where this connects to the course

KV caching and sparse attention are in Lecture 6. Top-k routing over experts is exactly what you implement in Assignment 2, Part 1.

§3The puzzle, and the gap

Does the BM25 correction help when the vocabulary is learned instead of made of words? Published results say "sometimes, by a lot" and "sometimes it hurts, by a lot".

Vocabulary (Clavié et al., App. C)BM25Inner productWinner
SAE features of a frozen retriever0.5820.381BM25, by 20 points
SPLADE-v30.4550.568inner product, by 11 points

Same benchmark suite, same scorers. Only the vocabulary changed, and the ranking of the two scorers flipped. Two other papers point the same way. Park et al. (CL-SR) lose 11–18% nDCG@10 when they remove IDF. Han et al., working on image patches, find the scorers roughly level, and when they raise the number of active features from 16 to 128, R@1 drops from 0.508 to 0.038. So the shape of the frequency distribution clearly matters.

The only explanation in print is qualitative: BM25 helps as long as the vocabulary stays Zipfian (a few features very common, a long tail of rare ones). The gap has three parts:

  1. That account rests on a handful of vocabularies, none of them varied under control. Nobody knows which property of a vocabulary does the work.
  2. It is qualitative, so for a new representation the only way to choose a scorer is to build both and test.
  3. It has never been tested inside a model, where frequency and usefulness may disagree. A KV page that is selected all the time may be an attention sink the model needs. A rarely used expert may be a "super expert" whose removal breaks the model.
what the project claims to add

The Zipfian explanation is not ours, and we don't claim it. We add a controlled family of vocabularies, a scorer whose parts switch independently, a number you can compute before running the experiment that is supposed to predict the effect, and a test of that number outside retrieval.

§4The problem, stated precisely

§4.1 · Representation and budget

A representation R maps an item (query or document) to a sparse non-negative vector φR(x) over m features. A budget (kq, kd) keeps only the largest kq coordinates of a query and kd of a document. The main budget is 32 / 128. Keeping the budget fixed means every scorer sees the same number of active features, so no scorer wins by seeing more.

§4.2 · Collection statistics

For a corpus of N documents, dfj is how many documents have feature j active. A document's length is |d| = Σj φj(d), and avgdl is the mean length. We use the smoothed IDF:

idfj=ln(1+N−dfj+0.5dfj+0.5)

The "1 +" inside the log keeps it strictly positive. Multiplying a score by it never flips its sign, which plain textbook IDF can do for features in more than half the documents.

§4.3 · The switchable scorer

A configuration σ = (σI, σS, σL) is three bits: IDF, saturation, length. The length factor is ℓ(d) = 1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl when σL = 1, and 1 otherwise. The weight of feature j in document d is

gjσ(d)=idfjσI⋅{(k1+1)φj(d)φj(d)+k1ℓ(d)σS=1φj(d)/ℓ(d)σS=0

and the score is sσ(q,d)=∑jφj(q)gjσ(d), with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75 (the standard BM25 defaults). In plain words: the query side stays as it is, and each document feature gets optionally reweighted by IDF, optionally squashed, and optionally divided by relative length.

why eight switches instead of two scorers

Comparing only the inner product and BM25 tells you whether the bundle helps, not which part. With all eight corners of the 2 × 2 × 2 cube you can measure each component's own effect. That is how we found out that IDF is the smallest part (§9.4).

§4.4 · The margin: the number we try to predict

Let Qσ(R, c) be the quality of configuration σ for representation R on collection c. For retrieval that is mean nDCG@10. The target is

m(R,c)=Q111−Q000sdσQσ

That is, the BM25-minus-inner-product gain, divided by the standard deviation of quality across the eight configurations. Dividing removes units. A margin of 2 means the same thing whether the metric is nDCG@10, RULER accuracy or MMLU accuracy, and that is what lets a predictor fitted on retrieval be scored on attention or routing. This choice was made in response to TA feedback on the interim proposal, which pointed out that the proposal never said what number the predictor outputs outside retrieval.

§4.5 · Diagnostics and the task

From the uncorrected activations of R on corpus c we compute a vector z of cheap statistics (§6). No relevance labels go into z. The task is to learn f with f(z) ≈ m, such that f:

  1. predicts the sign and size of m on basis families and corpora it has not seen,
  2. beats a baseline that only knows which corpus a row comes from,
  3. when carried without refitting to page selection and routing, keeps sign accuracy ≥ 70% and mean absolute error within 1.5× its retrieval error. These thresholds were fixed before any transformer run.
what one row is

One row of the regression is a (representation, collection) pair, not a representation. The same SAE has a different frequency curve on MS MARCO than on Telugu MIRACL, so its diagnostics differ too. 14 representations × 13 collections = 182 rows. This also came from the interim feedback: counting only representations would have left 16 points for 6 regressors.

§5What we built: Setting A, retrieval

§5.1 · One frozen encoder

Every representation starts from multilingual E5-base, run frozen in fp16 with max length 512. A pooled embedding is the mean over content tokens (skipping the prefix, special tokens and padding). The per-token states are kept for the token granularity. Holding the encoder fixed means any difference comes from how we build the vocabulary, not from a different model.

§5.2 · Seven bases in four families

A basis is a recipe that turns a dense vector into sparse non-negative features. All seven are fitted on 200,000 MS MARCO training passages (up to two million token states).

FamilyBasisIn plain words
LinearIdentityThe raw 768 E5 dimensions.
Gaussian random projectionMultiply by a random matrix to 768 dimensions.
PCAKeep the 384 directions of largest variance.
Clusteredk-means4,096 cluster centres. Each vector gets soft weights over its 4 nearest centres.
QuantisedProduct quantisation (PQ)Cut the vector into 8 pieces, replace each piece with the nearest of 256 codes. One-hot per piece.
Residual quantisation (RQ)8 stages of 256 codes, each stage encoding what the previous stages missed.
LearnedTop-K SAEA sparse autoencoder with 8,192 features, of which exactly 32 are active per input. Trained with AdamW for 3 epochs on reconstruction loss.
split rectification

Linear bases produce negative numbers, which BM25 can't handle. So each value z becomes two features, ([z]+, [−z]+): the positive part and the negative part. 768 signed numbers become 1,536 non-negative ones.

Each basis is used at two granularities. Pooled: a document is the code of its mean embedding. Token: a document is the sum of the codes of its tokens, in the spirit of ColBERT-style late interaction. 7 × 2 = 14 representations. SPLADE-v3 is kept as an outside reference only, since it isn't built from E5 and so doesn't belong to the controlled grid.

§5.4 · Scoring, indexing and the two reference points

Documents are transformed once per configuration and stored as compressed sparse columns (an inverted index). A query only touches the lists of documents for its active features, and retrieval is exact, with no approximation. There are two reference points outside the grid:

  • Lexical BM25 over ordinary words (in BM25S, with Unicode-aware tokens). This is the classic keyword baseline.
  • Dense E5 with exact search in FAISS on GPU. This is what the encoder does on its own, with no sparse step.

§6Diagnostics and the predictor

§6.1 · Six cheap measurements

One pass over the uncorrected, budget-truncated codes of a corpus gives six numbers. None of them needs a query or a relevance label.

#DiagnosticWhat it measures
iActive fractionWhat share of all features are ever switched on.
iiZipf exponent ★Slope of log(selection rate) against log(rank). How steep the frequency curve is.
iiiGini coefficientHow unequally activation mass is spread over features.
ivHead frequency massShare of selections taken by the top 1% most frequent features.
vIDF variance ★How much IDF can reweight at all. If all IDFs are equal, IDF does nothing.
viFrequent-activation share ★Share of activation mass held by that same top 1%.

★ = one of the three primary regressors, chosen before any margin was seen.

why only three

The 182 rows aren't independent. They cluster into just 4 basis families and 11 corpora. Six correlated regressors against that few groups would overfit easily. So we committed in advance to three: the shape of the curve (Zipf), how much IDF can do (IDF variance) and where the mass sits (frequent share). All six enter a secondary model.

§6.2 · Fitting and validation

The predictor is ridge regression (linear regression with a penalty on large weights) on standardised diagnostics. The penalty is picked from {0.1, 1, 10, 100} by an inner split that never sees the held-out group. We validate three ways, each answering a different question:

AxisHeld outCompared withQuestion
Familyeach basis family in turnthe mean margin of the row's corpusDoes it work on a new kind of basis?
Corpuseach corpus (the 3 MS MARCO sets as one)the training meanDoes it work on a new corpus?
Languagetrain on English, predict Indicthe training meanDoes it cross languages?

Reported: sign accuracy, AUC, held-out R² and its gain over the baseline, mean absolute error, and Kendall's τ between predicted and true margins within each collection. Intervals come from a two-way bootstrap that resamples corpora and representations together. The final model is refitted on all rows and frozen. Settings B and C load it read-only.

§6.3 · Significance, ablations and sanity controls

  • Per-margin uncertainty: a paired bootstrap over queries (2,000 draws) gives each margin an interval and a p-value.
  • Component effects are factorial main effects: for IDF, the mean quality of the four configurations with IDF on minus the mean of the four with it off. Same for saturation and length.
  • Holm correction adjusts p-values within each family of tests, because running 182 tests makes some small p-values happen by chance.
  • Controls: shuffled IDF keeps the IDF values but assigns them to the wrong features. Constant length and uniform frequency are synthetic corpora on which length normalisation and IDF must leave the ranking unchanged. The code asserts that they do.

§7Settings B and C: inside a transformer

Both adapters are built and pass their tests. Neither has run at scale yet. They are the October work.

§7.1 · Setting B, KV-page selection (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct)

During long-context decoding, attending to every cached key is expensive. Quest groups the keys into pages of 16 tokens and stores each page's elementwise min and max key. For the current query q it computes an upper bound on how much attention the page could get, Σi max(qi·mini, qi·maxi), and keeps the best pages until a budget of 512 or 1,024 tokens is full. The current page is always kept.

Our corrected version treats pages as documents:

  • The Quest score goes through a softplus first, so it is positive, then through the BM25 scorer.
  • A page's length is the norm of its summary.
  • A page's df is how often it entered the top-k across 64 positions sampled from the prefill. Pages created during decoding have no history, so they get a neutral IDF of 1.
  • Llama-3.2 uses grouped-query attention, so statistics are kept per query head over the shared keys.

Baselines are full attention, plain Quest and H2O (keep recent tokens plus "heavy hitters"). The benchmark is 13 RULER tasks at 4K and 8K context. The full cache stays in memory, so we report how much of the KV cache was selected and make no claim about speed.

the sign question

H2O is the closest existing method to a frequency statistic, but it uses accumulated attention to keep items. Our correction uses selection frequency to discount them, the opposite sign. A planned sink ablation tests which sign is right.

§7.2 · Setting C, expert routing (OLMoE-1B-7B)

OLMoE has 64 experts per layer with 8 active per token. That is enough experts for a frequency curve to have a shape. It runs in fp16 split across two GPUs. We replace the router's top-k with a top-k over corrected scores:

  • The router probabilities are the features.
  • An expert's df is the number of tokens that picked it over 256 calibration documents of 2,048 tokens. Length is the norm of the router logits.
  • Only which experts are picked changes. The chosen experts are mixed with their original probabilities.
  • Configuration 000 calls the native forward pass, so it matches the unmodified model exactly.

Two extra modes:

  • Frequency bias: add λ(k/E − p̂e) to each expert's probability, where p̂e is its calibration selection rate. Overused experts get pushed down and underused ones up. This is an inference-time version of loss-free balancing.
  • Super-expert protection: never demote the experts with the largest output norm. This tests the finding that a few rare experts are critical.

Quality: MMLU accuracy (primary), GSM8K accuracy and WikiText log-likelihood.

§8Cases where we know the answer in advance

Some rows have margins fixed by pure maths. They are a free end-to-end check of the pipeline, and the regression has to treat them differently because they carry no information about size.

§8.1 · Constant statistics

If every feature has the same df, IDF multiplies everything by the same number and can't change a ranking. If every document has the same length, ℓ(d) = 1 and length normalisation does nothing. The synthetic controls check both.

§8.2 · Pooled PQ and RQ: the margin must be ±2

At pooled granularity, PQ and RQ give exactly one active code per piece, all with the same value v. So every document has the same length, and saturation maps every active v to the same constant. Saturation and length then only rescale all scores by one common factor, which leaves the ranking unchanged. Only IDF can change anything. The eight configurations collapse into two groups of four:

the arithmetic

Four configurations score Q000 and four score Q111. Call the gap Δ. The mean is the midpoint, every value is Δ/2 away from it, so sd = |Δ|/2 and m = Δ / (|Δ|/2) = ±2, whatever the corpus.

Pooled k-means has soft weights that always sum to one, so its lengths are constant too and its eight configurations reduce to four.

§8.3 · The margin can never exceed 4

For any n numbers with population standard deviation s, two of them can differ by at most s√(2n), because those two alone contribute at least (xi − xj)²/2n to the variance. With n = 8, √16 = 4, so |m| ≤ 4 for every row. The pooled quantisation rows sit at exactly half the bound and only tell you the sign.

§8.4 · Linear bases

For a linear basis u = Cx without rectification, IDF weighting gives the score xq⊤C⊤DC xd with D = diag(idf). That ranks the same as the dense dot product for every query only if C⊤DC is a multiple of the identity. Split rectification breaks this identity, which is why the linear bases still show IDF effects.

§9Results for Setting A

§9.1 · What ran

All 182 primary rows finished under all eight configurations, plus 26 reference runs (BM25 and dense E5 on 13 collections). We looked at the grid only after the last row finished. Not run yet: the seed, budget and k1/b families, and Settings B and C at scale.

§9.2 · The correction almost always helps

Of the 179 rows with a defined margin (three have Q identical across configurations), 161 are positive. At token granularity, 88 of 91 are. Every negative margin comes from a raw change smaller than 0.01 nDCG@10. Twelve of the 18 negatives are pooled k-means or pooled PQ, whose codes give the correction almost nothing to reweight, and five more are on LIMIT. So full BM25 never costs a meaningful amount, and its biggest gains are close to 0.5 nDCG@10.

§9.3 · The exact predictions hold

Pooled PQ and RQ give margins of exactly ±2 on all 12 collections where the margin is defined, as §8.2 predicts. (On LIMIT every configuration scores zero for them, so the margin is undefined.) Their saturation and length effects are exactly zero on all 13 collections, and so is the length effect of pooled k-means. These numbers come out of the full pipeline, from encoding through evaluation, so they check all of it.

The pre-registered predictor fails its own test. Its sign accuracy (0.899, and 0.976 on Indic) is exactly what you get by always guessing "positive". Its held-out R² is near zero. It does beat the baseline R² by 0.14, 0.04 and 0.10, but only because those baselines are below zero. The inner search chose the strongest penalty on offer (100), and the frozen model is basically "predict 1.92", with no coefficient above 0.27 per standard deviation. The six-feature and precision-weighted variants are no better (R² from −0.17 to 0.12).

why: the target saturates

142 of the 179 margins sit between 2 and 3.2. Here is why. Suppose the inner product (000) scores Δ below the other seven, and those seven are all about equal. Then the mean is Δ/8 below the seven, the population variance is 7Δ²/64, and so sd = Δ√7/8. The margin is

m = Δ / (Δ√7/8) = 8/√7 ≈ 3.02

whatever the size of Δ. A gain of 0.01 and a gain of 0.5 get the same margin. So the margin mostly records one yes/no fact: is the inner product the odd one out among the eight? In this grid it almost always is. The variance left over comes from the structural ±2 rows and from rows with a near-zero raw change, where the sign is noise. No diagnostic can predict that.

The raw difference Q111 − Q000 (0 to 0.5) keeps the information the margin throws away. The flaw also weakens the transfer test, since "sign accuracy ≥ 70%" can be met by always predicting a gain. So before any transfer result exists, we add the majority-sign baseline to the transfer comparison.

§9.6 · A floor on the corrected p-values

With 2,000 bootstrap draws, the smallest possible p-value is 1/2001. Holm correction multiplies the smallest by 182, giving 182/2001 ≈ 0.091. So no primary comparison can be called significant at 0.05, however large the effect, and 134 of the 182 sit right at that floor. With 20,000 draws the floor drops to 0.009, which is the first job in October. The bootstrap intervals quoted above don't depend on this.

§10What happens next

§10.1 · What the results change

  • The frozen predictor stays frozen. It goes to Settings B and C exactly as registered, and every transfer result is reported next to the majority-sign baseline.
  • Any new target (the raw difference, or a single component effect) is labelled exploratory, because it was chosen after seeing the retrieval grid.
  • The sparsity family moves ahead of the other secondary runs, because it tests the one account of the component result that we have.

§11Risks and limitations

  • One encoder. All bases share E5, so basis effects and encoder effects can't be fully separated.
  • E5 has seen MIRACL training data. The Indic rows say nothing about languages the encoder has never seen. Our claims are about the fitted bases, the scoring and the prediction.
  • Setting B keeps the whole cache in memory, so it says nothing about speed or memory savings.
  • Setting C claims nothing about training, only inference-time routing.
  • One budget. Every retrieval number uses 32/128 features. IDF's small role may not hold at larger budgets, where document frequencies spread out.
  • Transfer inherits the margin's weakness, which is why it will sit next to the majority-sign baseline.
the outcome worth hoping for

If the correction helps retrieval but hurts inside a model, that would be the most informative result of all. It would mean frequency and importance really do come apart in the transformer, for example because the most-selected pages are attention sinks the model needs.

§12Engineering for the hardware we had

Nodes have four 11 GB GPUs, with no bf16 and no FlashAttention-2. The full grid takes days, so every stage writes a manifest (config hash, parent checksums, code version), resumes from checkpoints, and verifies checksums before reusing anything. Three bottlenecks each needed a fix.

BottleneckProblemFixEffect
EncodingThe E5 forward pass over MS MARCO was repeated once per basis, 7×. Run E5 once per batch and apply every basis to the same GPU activations, sharded over 4 workers. 182 encoding tasks → 57 shared units. Full corpus encoding in under 7 hours.
PostingsPostings were re-transformed for every query batch under 8 configs. Cache transformed postings on local disk, keyed by parent artefact, statistics and switch setting. 1.1 s to load vs 7.4 s to rebuild. All 512 ranking comparisons matched exactly.
ScoringCPU scoring was the last bottleneck. GPU path: 512 queries per pass, fp32, products added in feature order without fused multiply-add, ties broken by document ID. 24.0 s → 1.8 s for 512 queries. Matched CPU exactly on 960 comparisons.

Two smaller details: encoding batch size stays at 8, because larger batches moved some quantised assignments past our numerical tolerance. Exact dense search over 8.8M passages doesn't fit on one 11 GB card, so it runs over shards on the GPU and merges the per-shard results.

why so much care about exact matches

With top-k, a tiny floating-point difference can swap two documents near the cut-off and change nDCG. If the fast GPU path didn't reproduce the CPU rankings exactly, we couldn't tell a real effect of a switch from arithmetic noise. That is why the GPU path fixes the order of additions and the tie-breaking.

§15Viva questions

Likely questions about the mid submission, with short answers you can say out loud.

  1. Explain the project in two sentences.

    "Retrieval, KV-page selection and MoE routing all take a dot product against non-negative features and keep the top k, and none of them uses the collection frequency statistics BM25 is built on. We test when BM25's corrections help over a controlled family of learned vocabularies, try to predict that from label-free statistics, and then carry the predictor into attention and routing."

  2. Why eight configurations and not just BM25 versus inner product?

    "Because BM25 is three changes bundled together. With each switched independently we get a 2³ factorial design, so we can attribute the gain to a component. That's how we found saturation does most of the work and IDF the least."

  3. Why divide the gain by the standard deviation over configurations?

    "To make it unitless, so the same predictor output means the same thing for nDCG, RULER accuracy and MMLU. The downside, which we found, is that it saturates: when the inner product is the only outlier the margin is 8/√7 ≈ 3.02 regardless of the size of the gain. It's also bounded by 4."

  4. Why is the pooled PQ margin exactly ±2?

    "Pooled PQ has one active code per subspace, all with the same value, so every document has the same length and saturation maps every value to the same constant. Saturation and length only rescale scores uniformly, so only IDF changes the ranking. Four configs score Q000 and four score Q111, the sd is half the gap, so the margin is ±2. We got exactly that on all 12 collections, which checks the pipeline end to end."

  5. Your predictor failed. Why should we believe anything else?

    "The failure is in the target, not the retrieval grid. The raw results stand on their own: 161 of 179 positive margins, the exact ±2 checks, and the component split with bootstrap intervals. We traced the failure to the saturated margin, kept the frozen predictor as registered for transfer, added a majority-sign baseline, and any new target is labelled exploratory."

  6. Why is IDF so weak here when CL-SR loses up to 18% without it?

    "At a budget of 128 document features, top-k truncation already removes most of the very common features IDF would down-weight. That is Han et al.'s account. The sparsity family varies the budget to test it directly, which is why we moved it forward."

  7. Why does the inner product over token-level SAE codes score so badly?

    "A token-level document is the sum of its tokens' codes, so features that fire on many tokens get huge values and dominate the dot product. That is the case saturation was designed for, and the SAE token saturation effect is the largest in the table at 19.3 points."

  8. How do you get df for KV pages and experts, which aren't documents?

    "We use selection counts. For a page, how many of 64 sampled prefill positions put it in the top-k. For an expert, how many tokens chose it over 256 calibration documents. The scores are passed through a positive map (softplus for Quest, router probabilities for OLMoE) so saturation is defined."

  9. Why is nothing significant after Holm correction?

    "With 2,000 bootstrap draws the smallest p-value is 1/2001. Holm multiplies it by 182, so the floor is 0.091. That's a resolution limit, not evidence of no effect, and 134 rows sit at it. We're rerunning with 20,000 draws, which lowers the floor to 0.009."

  10. What is the biggest limitation?

    "Everything comes from one encoder at one budget. So we can't fully separate basis from encoder, and IDF's small role might change at larger budgets. Also, E5 has seen MIRACL training data, so the Indic results aren't about unseen languages."
