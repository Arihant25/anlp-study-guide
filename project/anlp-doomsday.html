When does counting help you choose?

Our project, Predicting When Collection Statistics Help Top-k Selection: From Retrieval to Attention and Expert Routing, asks whether an old idea from search engines can improve three quite different parts of modern NLP systems, and whether we can tell in advance when it will. This page walks through the mid report from the ground up: the idea, what we built, what the experiments showed, and the part that did not work.

mid · 30 Sep 2026 182 retrieval rows 7 bases · 13 collections BM25 · Quest · OLMoE

Aviral Gupta · Mohit Kumar Singh · Anirudh Sankar · Arihant Tripathy — IIIT Hyderabad

§0The short version

A search engine, the attention layer of a language model and the router in a mixture-of-experts model look like unrelated pieces of machinery, but each of them does the same two things. It scores an input against a set of features using a dot product, and then it keeps only the k highest-scoring items. Search engines learnt long ago that the raw dot product can be improved with a correction called BM25, which takes into account how common each feature is across the whole collection. The other two mechanisms have never used it. We wanted to know when that correction actually helps, and whether it is possible to predict the answer before running the experiment.

BM25 is really three corrections bundled together. IDF gives rare features more weight than common ones, saturation stops a feature from counting ever more as it repeats, and length normalisation keeps long documents from winning simply because they are long. We turned each of these into an independent on/off switch, which gives eight scorers ranging from the plain dot product (everything off) to full BM25 (everything on). We then built fourteen sparse "concept" vocabularies from a single frozen encoder, multilingual E5, and scored every one of them on thirteen English and Indic retrieval collections under all eight scorers. The same scorer is also wired into KV-cache page selection on Llama-3.2-1B and expert routing on OLMoE. Those two adapters are built and tested, but they have not yet been run at scale.

The retrieval grid is complete, and four findings came out of it. The full correction improves retrieval in 161 of 179 cases and never costs more than 0.01 nDCG@10, with the largest gains close to 0.5. Most of that gain comes from saturation and length normalisation, while IDF, the collection statistic the project set out to study, contributes the least and is the only component that ever hurts. A handful of results can be worked out on paper in advance, and every one of them comes out exactly as predicted, which gives us confidence in the pipeline. Finally, the predictor we committed to before seeing the data does no better than guessing "the correction helps" every time. We traced that failure to the way we normalised the quantity it predicts, and §9.5 explains the cause and how the plan changes because of it.

§1Background you need

§1.1 · Retrieval as a dot product

A retriever is given a query and a large pile of documents, called the collection or corpus, and has to return the documents most likely to answer the query, in order. Most modern retrievers do this by turning the query into a vector uq and every document into a vector ud. Each document is scored with the dot product ⟨uq, ud⟩, and the top k are returned.

These vectors come in two broad kinds. A dense retriever such as DPR or E5 produces vectors of a few hundred numbers, all of them active and any of them possibly negative. A sparse retriever produces much longer vectors, with one slot per feature, where almost every slot is zero and the few non-zero ones are positive. Traditional keyword search is the classic sparse retriever, with one slot for each word in the vocabulary. Learned sparse retrieval keeps that sparse format but lets a model decide what the features should be, as SPLADE and the recent SAE-based retrievers do. This project works within that family.

§1.2 · What BM25 adds to the dot product

BM25 is the classical scoring function for keyword search, and after several decades it remains a strong baseline. It improves on the plain dot product with three corrections, each aimed at a particular way in which raw counts give a misleading picture of relevance.

ComponentThe problem it fixesWhat it does
IDF
inverse document frequency		 A word like "the" appears in every document, so matching it tells you nothing. Multiplies each feature by a weight that is large when few documents contain it and near zero when all of them do.
Saturation A document that says "cat" 50 times is not 50 times more about cats than one that says it once. Passes each feature's value through a curve that rises quickly and then flattens out, capped at k1+1.
Length normalisation Long documents match more query terms simply because they contain more text. Divides by the document's length relative to the average.
a worked example

Suppose the collection has a million documents. A feature that appears in only 10 of them receives an IDF weight of about 11.5, one that appears in half of them receives ln 2 ≈ 0.69, and one that appears in every document receives about 0.0000005, which effectively removes it from the score.

For saturation, take the standard setting k1 = 1.2 and a document of average length. A raw value of 1 stays at 1.00, a value of 5 becomes 1.77, and a value of 50 becomes only 2.15. However often a feature repeats, its contribution can never exceed 2.2.

For BM25 to make sense, a representation has to provide three things. Its values must be non-negative, since saturating a negative number has no meaning. Each feature must have a meaningful count of the documents it appears in, which is what IDF is computed from. And each document must have a length. A dense embedding provides none of these, because every coordinate is active in every document and any of them can be negative, which is why dense retrievers have always stuck to the plain dot product. A sparse representation, by contrast, provides all three.

§1.3 · Concept vocabularies from a dense encoder

There are several ways to turn a dense embedding into sparse features. You can cluster the embeddings and use cluster membership as the features, compress them into discrete codes, or train a sparse autoencoder (SAE), a small network that rewrites each vector as a handful of active features chosen from several thousand. Several recent papers do exactly this and then treat the resulting features as a vocabulary for retrieval. We use seven such constructions, which §5 describes.

§2The observation: three things that are one thing

The whole project rests on one observation. Three mechanisms, developed by three different research communities, perform the same computation: they give each feature a non-negative score and then make a hard top-k selection.

SettingThe "query"The "documents"What is kept
A · Learned sparse retrievalquery vector uq document vectors udtop-k documents
B · KV-page selection (Quest)current attention query qt summaries of pages of 16 cached keystop pages to attend to
C · MoE routingtoken hidden state ht expert embeddings wetop-k experts

None of the three takes into account how often a feature is selected across the whole collection, even though that is exactly the statistic IDF is built on. Fragments of the idea have nonetheless surfaced inside transformers under other names. Loss-free balancing, a technique for training mixture-of-experts models, adds a bias to each expert that shrinks when the expert is used too often, which amounts to a frequency discount applied during training. StreamingLLM always keeps the first few tokens in the KV cache because they absorb a large share of attention, a phenomenon known as the attention sink. Each is a local fix for one mechanism, and neither is framed as a general correction.

KV caching and sparse attention are covered in Lecture 6, and top-k routing over experts is what you implement in Assignment 2, Part 1.

§3The puzzle, and the gap

Does the BM25 correction still help when the vocabulary is learned rather than made of words? The published evidence is contradictory: sometimes it helps a great deal, and sometimes it does real harm.

Vocabulary (Clavié et al., App. C)BM25Inner productWinner
SAE features of a frozen retriever0.5820.381BM25, by 20 points
SPLADE-v30.4550.568inner product, by 11 points

Both rows use the same benchmark suite and the same two scorers. The only thing that changed is the vocabulary, and that alone was enough to reverse which scorer comes out ahead. Other work points the same way. Park et al. (CL-SR) lose 11 to 18 percent of their nDCG@10 when they remove IDF. Han et al., working with image patches, find the two scorers roughly level, and when they raise the number of active features from 16 to 128, R@1 falls from 0.508 to 0.038. Whatever decides the outcome, it clearly has to do with how feature frequencies are distributed.

The only explanation offered so far is qualitative: BM25 helps as long as the vocabulary stays Zipfian, meaning a few features are very common and there is a long tail of rare ones, as with words in ordinary text. That account leaves three things open. It is based on a handful of vocabularies that were never varied in a controlled way, so nobody knows which property of a vocabulary is doing the work. Because it is qualitative, it gives a practitioner with a new representation no way to choose a scorer other than building both and comparing them. And it has never been tested inside a model, where frequency and usefulness may well diverge: a KV page that is selected constantly could be an attention sink the model depends on, and a rarely used expert could be one of the "super experts" whose removal breaks the model.

what this project adds

The Zipfian explanation belongs to earlier work, and we do not claim it. What we contribute is a controlled family of vocabularies, a scorer whose components can be switched independently, a quantity that can be computed before running an experiment and is meant to predict its outcome, and a test of that prediction outside retrieval.

§4The problem, stated precisely

§4.1 · Representation and budget

A representation R maps an item, whether a query or a document, to a sparse non-negative vector φR(x) over m features. To keep comparisons fair we impose a budget (kq, kd): only the largest kq coordinates of a query and the largest kd of a document are kept. The main budget is 32 for queries and 128 for documents. Because the budget is the same for every scorer, no scorer can win simply by seeing more features than another.

§4.2 · Collection statistics

For a corpus of N documents, dfj is the number of documents in which feature j is active. A document's length is the sum of its feature values, |d| = Σj φj(d), and avgdl is the average length across the corpus. We use a smoothed form of IDF:

idfj=ln(1+N−dfj+0.5dfj+0.5)

The "1 +" inside the logarithm keeps the weight strictly positive. The textbook form of IDF turns negative for features that occur in more than half the documents, and multiplying by it would flip the sign of a score. This form never does.

§4.3 · The switchable scorer

A configuration σ = (σI, σS, σL) is a set of three bits that switch IDF, saturation and length normalisation on or off. When length normalisation is on, the length factor is ℓ(d) = 1 − b + b·|d|/avgdl, and otherwise it is simply 1. The weight given to feature j in document d is then

gjσ(d)=idfjσI⋅{(k1+1)φj(d)φj(d)+k1ℓ(d)σS=1φj(d)/ℓ(d)σS=0

and the score is sσ(q,d)=∑jφj(q)gjσ(d), with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75, the standard BM25 defaults. Put simply, the query side is left as it is, while each document feature is optionally reweighted by IDF, optionally passed through the saturation curve, and optionally divided by the document's relative length.

why eight configurations rather than two

Comparing only the inner product with BM25 would tell us whether the bundle helps, but not which part of it is responsible. Running all eight corners of the 2 × 2 × 2 cube lets us measure the effect of each component on its own, and that is how we found that IDF contributes the least (§9.4).

§4.4 · The margin: the number we try to predict

Let Qσ(R, c) be the quality of configuration σ for representation R on collection c. In retrieval this is mean nDCG@10. The quantity we want to predict is

m(R,c)=Q111−Q000sdσQσ

that is, the gain of full BM25 over the plain inner product, divided by the standard deviation of quality across all eight configurations. The division makes the margin unitless, so a margin of 2 carries the same meaning whether the underlying metric is nDCG@10, RULER accuracy or MMLU accuracy, and a predictor fitted on retrieval can therefore be scored on attention or routing. We adopted this definition after the TA's feedback on the interim proposal, which pointed out that we had never said what number the predictor would produce outside retrieval.

§4.5 · Diagnostics and the task

From the uncorrected activations of R on corpus c we compute a small vector z of cheap statistics, described in §6. No relevance judgements are used anywhere in z. The task is to learn a function f with f(z) ≈ m, and we set ourselves three criteria for success. The predictor should get the sign and size of m right on basis families and corpora it has never seen. It should beat a baseline that knows only which corpus a row comes from. And when it is carried, without refitting, to page selection and routing, it should keep a sign accuracy of at least 70% and a mean absolute error no more than 1.5 times its error on retrieval. These thresholds were fixed before any transformer experiment was run.

what counts as one data point

Each row of the regression is a (representation, collection) pair rather than a representation, because the same SAE has a different frequency curve on MS MARCO than on Telugu MIRACL and therefore different diagnostics. Fourteen representations across thirteen collections gives 182 rows. This framing also came from the interim feedback, which noted that counting only representations would have left 16 data points to fit 6 regressors.

§5What we built: Setting A, retrieval

§5.1 · One frozen encoder

Every representation starts from multilingual E5-base, run frozen in fp16 with a maximum input length of 512. A pooled embedding is the mean over the content tokens, leaving out the prefix, special tokens and padding, and the individual token states are kept as well for the token-level variant. Holding the encoder fixed is what makes the comparison controlled: any difference between two representations comes from how the vocabulary was built, not from a different model underneath.

§5.2 · Seven bases in four families

A basis is a recipe for turning a dense vector into sparse, non-negative features. All seven were fitted on 200,000 MS MARCO training passages, which amounts to up to two million token states.

FamilyBasisHow it works

Residual quantisation (RQ)Uses 8 stages of 256 codes, where each stage encodes what the earlier stages missed.
LearnedTop-K SAEA sparse autoencoder with 8,192 features, exactly 32 of which are active for any input, trained with AdamW for three epochs on reconstruction loss.
split rectification

The linear bases produce negative values, which BM25 cannot handle. We therefore split each value z into two features, ([z]+, [−z]+), holding its positive and negative parts separately. The 768 signed numbers become 1,536 non-negative ones.

Each basis is used at two granularities. At the pooled level, a document is represented by the code of its mean embedding. At the token level, it is represented by the sum of the codes of all its tokens, in the spirit of late-interaction models such as ColBERT. Seven bases at two granularities give fourteen representations. SPLADE-v3 is kept only as an outside reference: it is not built from E5, so it falls outside the controlled grid.

§5.4 · Scoring, indexing and the two reference points

Documents are transformed once for each configuration and stored as compressed sparse columns, which is an inverted index: a query only has to visit the lists of documents for its own active features. Retrieval is exact, with no approximation. Two reference systems sit outside the grid. Lexical BM25, run in BM25S over ordinary Unicode-aware word tokens, is the classic keyword baseline. Dense E5, searched exactly with FAISS on GPU, shows what the encoder achieves on its own without any sparse step in between.

§6Diagnostics and the predictor

§6.1 · Six cheap measurements

A single pass over the uncorrected, budget-truncated codes of a corpus produces six numbers. None of them requires a query or a relevance label, so they can be computed for any new representation before any evaluation is done.

#DiagnosticWhat it measures
iActive fractionThe share of all features that are ever switched on.
iiZipf exponent ★The slope of log selection rate against log rank, which describes how steep the frequency curve is.
iiiGini coefficientHow unequally activation mass is spread across features.
ivHead frequency massThe share of selections taken by the 1% most frequent features.
vIDF variance ★How much room IDF has to reweight anything. If every IDF were equal, it would change nothing.
viFrequent-activation share ★The share of activation mass, rather than selections, held by that same top 1%.

★ marks the three primary regressors, which were chosen before any margin had been seen.

The reason for restricting the main model to three regressors is that the 182 rows are far from independent. They cluster into just four basis families and eleven corpora, and fitting six correlated regressors to so few genuine groups would overfit easily. We therefore committed in advance to three diagnostics that each capture something different: the shape of the frequency curve (the Zipf exponent), how much IDF can do at all (IDF variance), and where the activation mass is concentrated (the frequent-activation share). All six enter a secondary model.

§6.2 · Fitting and validation

The predictor is ridge regression, which is linear regression with a penalty on large weights, fitted on standardised diagnostics. The penalty is chosen from {0.1, 1, 10, 100} by an inner split that never sees the held-out group. We validate it along three axes, each of which answers a different question about generalisation:

AxisHeld outCompared withQuestion
Familyeach basis family in turnthe mean margin of the row's corpusDoes it work on a new kind of basis?
Corpuseach corpus (the 3 MS MARCO sets as one)the training meanDoes it work on a new corpus?

For each axis we report sign accuracy, AUC, held-out R² together with its improvement over the baseline, mean absolute error, and Kendall's τ between predicted and true margins within each collection. Uncertainty intervals come from a two-way bootstrap that resamples corpora and representations together. Once validation is done, the final model is refitted on all rows and frozen, and Settings B and C load it read-only so that nothing learned from them can leak back into it.

§6.3 · Significance, ablations and sanity controls

Every individual margin gets its own interval and p-value from a paired bootstrap over queries with 2,000 draws. The contribution of each component is measured as a factorial main effect: for IDF, that is the average quality of the four configurations with IDF switched on minus the average of the four with it off, and saturation and length are treated the same way. Because we run 182 tests, some small p-values would appear by chance alone, so we apply Holm correction within each family of tests.

Three controls check that the mechanism behaves as it should. The first, shuffled IDF, keeps the IDF values but assigns them to the wrong features, which tests whether the benefit depends on the right features being reweighted. The other two are synthetic corpora, one where every document has the same length and one where every feature has the same frequency. On these, length normalisation and IDF respectively must leave the ranking unchanged, and the code asserts that they do.

§7Settings B and C: inside a transformer

Both adapters are built and pass their tests, but neither has been run at scale yet. That work is scheduled for October.

§7.1 · Setting B, KV-page selection (Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct)

When a model decodes over a long context, attending to every cached key at every step is expensive. Quest reduces the cost by grouping the keys into pages of 16 tokens and storing the elementwise minimum and maximum key of each page. For the current query q it computes an upper bound on the attention a page could receive, Σi max(qi·mini, qi·maxi), and then takes whole pages in order of that bound until a budget of 512 or 1,024 tokens is filled. The page currently being written is always kept.

Our corrected version treats pages as documents. The Quest score is first passed through a softplus so that it is positive, and then through the BM25 scorer. A page's length is the norm of its summary, and its df is the number of times it entered the top-k across 64 positions sampled from the prefill. Pages created during decoding have no history, so they receive a neutral IDF of 1. Because Llama-3.2 uses grouped-query attention, these statistics are kept separately for each query head over the shared keys.

We compare against full attention, plain Quest, and H2O, which keeps recent tokens together with the "heavy hitters" that have accumulated the most attention. The benchmark is thirteen RULER tasks at 4K and 8K context. Since the full cache stays in memory throughout, we report how much of it was selected and make no claims about speed.

which direction the correction should push

H2O is the existing method closest to a frequency statistic, but it uses accumulated attention to decide which items to keep. Our correction uses selection frequency in the opposite direction, to discount items that are selected often. A planned sink ablation will test which of the two directions is right.

§7.2 · Setting C, expert routing (OLMoE-1B-7B)

OLMoE has 64 experts per layer, of which 8 are active for each token, and that is enough experts for their selection frequencies to form a meaningful curve. The model runs in fp16, split across two GPUs. We replace the router's top-k with a top-k over corrected scores, using the router probabilities as the features. An expert's df is the number of tokens that selected it across 256 calibration documents of 2,048 tokens each, and its length is the norm of the router logits. Only the choice of experts changes: the selected experts are still mixed using their original probabilities. The all-off configuration calls the model's native forward pass, so it reproduces the unmodified model exactly.

Two further modes test specific ideas from the literature. The frequency-bias mode adds λ(k/E − p̂e) to each expert's probability, where p̂e is its selection rate on the calibration set, so that overused experts are pushed down and underused ones pushed up. This is an inference-time counterpart of loss-free balancing. The protection mode never demotes the experts with the largest output norms, which tests the finding that a few rarely used experts are critical to the model. Quality is measured mainly by MMLU accuracy, with GSM8K accuracy and WikiText log-likelihood as secondary measures.

§8Cases where we know the answer in advance

For some rows of the grid, the margin is fixed by the mathematics alone. These rows give us a free end-to-end check of the pipeline, and the regression has to treat them with care, because they carry information about the sign of the effect but not its size.

§8.1 · Constant statistics

If every feature has the same df, IDF multiplies every score by the same number and cannot change a ranking. Likewise, if every document has the same length, ℓ(d) = 1 and length normalisation has no effect. The synthetic controls described in §6.3 check both of these.

§8.2 · Pooled PQ and RQ: the margin must be ±2

At the pooled level, PQ and RQ produce exactly one active code per piece, and all of these codes have the same value v. Every document therefore has the same length, and saturation maps every active v to the same constant. As a result, saturation and length can only rescale all scores by a common factor, which leaves the ranking untouched, and IDF is the only switch that can make any difference. The eight configurations collapse into two groups of four.

the arithmetic

Four configurations score Q000 and the other four score Q111. If we call the gap between them Δ, the mean lies at the midpoint, every value is Δ/2 away from it, and so the standard deviation is |Δ|/2. The margin is then m = Δ / (|Δ|/2) = ±2, whatever the corpus.

Pooled k-means has soft weights that always sum to one, so its document lengths are constant as well, and its eight configurations reduce to four.

§8.3 · The margin can never exceed 4

For any n numbers with population standard deviation s, no two of them can differ by more than s√(2n), because those two values alone contribute at least (xi − xj)²/2n to the variance. With n = 8 the bound is 4s, so |m| ≤ 4 for every row. The pooled quantisation rows sit at exactly half this bound, which is why they tell us only the sign of the effect.

§8.4 · Linear bases

For a linear basis u = Cx without rectification, IDF weighting turns the score into xq⊤C⊤DC xd, where D = diag(idf). This produces the same rankings as the dense dot product for every query only if C⊤DC is a multiple of the identity. Split rectification breaks this identity, which is why the linear bases still show IDF effects.

§9Results for Setting A

§9.1 · What ran

All 182 primary rows finished under all eight configurations, along with 26 reference runs (text BM25 and dense E5 on each of the thirteen collections). We did not look at the grid until the last row had finished. The seed, budget and k1/b families have not run yet, and neither have Settings B and C at scale.

§9.2 · The correction almost always helps

Of the 179 rows where the margin is defined (in the other three, every configuration scores the same), 161 are positive, and at the token level the figure is 88 of 91. Every negative margin comes from a raw change smaller than 0.01 nDCG@10. Twelve of the eighteen negatives come from pooled k-means or pooled PQ, whose codes leave the correction almost nothing to reweight, and five more are on LIMIT. In other words, full BM25 never costs a meaningful amount of quality, while its largest gains approach 0.5 nDCG@10.

§9.3 · The exact predictions hold

Pooled PQ and RQ give margins of exactly ±2 on all twelve collections where the margin is defined, just as §8.2 predicts. (On LIMIT every configuration scores zero for them, so the margin is undefined.) Their saturation and length effects are exactly zero on all thirteen collections, and so is the length effect of pooled k-means. Because these values come out of the full pipeline, from encoding through to evaluation, they confirm that every stage of it is working as intended.

The bootstrap interval of the effect lies above zero in 132 rows for saturation, 118 for length and 97 for IDF. IDF is also the only component whose interval ever lies below zero, which happens in 14 rows. And when IDF is switched on last, after saturation and length are already on (going from 011 to 111), it actually lowers nDCG@10 in 99 of the 182 rows.

why this matters for the project

IDF is the one collection statistic that all three settings lack, and predicting its value was the point of the project. Yet at a budget of 128 features it turns out to be the smallest part of the gain. This fits the account of Han et al., in which a hard top-k has already removed most of the common features that IDF would otherwise down-weight. The sparsity family, which varies the budget, is the direct test of that account, and we have moved it to the front of the queue.

The predictor we registered in advance fails its own test. Its sign accuracy, 0.899 on families and corpora and 0.976 on the Indic rows, is exactly what you would get by always guessing that the correction helps, and its held-out R² is close to zero. It does beat the baseline R² by 0.14, 0.04 and 0.10, but only because those baselines are themselves below zero. The inner search chose the strongest penalty available, and the frozen model amounts to predicting a constant of 1.92, with no coefficient larger than 0.27 per standard deviation. The six-feature and precision-weighted variants do no better, with R² between −0.17 and 0.12.

why the target saturates

Of the 179 margins, 142 fall between 2 and 3.2, and the reason lies in how the margin is defined. Suppose the inner product (000) scores Δ below the other seven configurations, and those seven are roughly equal to one another. The mean then sits Δ/8 below the seven, the population variance is 7Δ²/64, and the standard deviation is Δ√7/8. The margin works out to

m = Δ / (Δ√7/8) = 8/√7 ≈ 3.02

regardless of how large Δ is, so a gain of 0.01 and a gain of 0.5 receive the same margin. What the margin really records, then, is whether the inner product is the odd one out among the eight configurations, and in this grid it nearly always is. The little variation that remains comes from the structural ±2 rows and from rows where the raw change is close to zero and its sign is essentially noise. No diagnostic could be expected to predict that.

The raw difference Q111 − Q000, which ranges from 0 to 0.5, keeps exactly the information the margin discards. The flaw also weakens the planned transfer test, since a sign-accuracy threshold of 70% can be met simply by predicting a gain everywhere. For that reason, and before any transfer result exists, we have added the majority-sign baseline to the transfer comparison.

§9.6 · A floor on the corrected p-values

With 2,000 bootstrap draws, the smallest p-value the procedure can produce is 1/2001. Holm correction multiplies the smallest p-value by 182, which gives a floor of 182/2001 ≈ 0.091. No primary comparison can therefore be declared significant at the 0.05 level, however large its effect, and 134 of the 182 sit right at that floor. Rerunning with 20,000 draws lowers the floor to 0.009, and that is the first task for October. The bootstrap intervals quoted earlier are not affected by this limit.

§10What happens next

§10.1 · What the results change

The frozen predictor stays frozen. It goes to Settings B and C exactly as it was registered, and every transfer result will be reported alongside the majority-sign baseline so that readers can see whether it adds anything. Any new target we try, such as the raw difference or the effect of a single component, will be labelled exploratory, because we chose it after seeing the retrieval results. The sparsity family moves ahead of the other secondary experiments, since it directly tests the one explanation we have for the component results.

§11Risks and limitations

The most basic limitation is that every basis is built on the same encoder, so the effect of the basis cannot be fully separated from the effect of E5 itself. E5 has also seen MIRACL training data, which means the Indic results say nothing about languages the encoder has never encountered; our claims concern the fitted bases, the scoring and the prediction, not the encoder's reach. In Setting B the whole cache remains in memory, so the experiments say nothing about savings in speed or memory, and Setting C concerns routing at inference time only, not training.

Every retrieval number so far also comes from a single budget of 32/128 features, and IDF's small role may not survive at larger budgets, where document frequencies spread out. Finally, any transfer result scored in margin units inherits the weakness described in §9.5, which is why it will always be reported next to the majority-sign baseline.

the most informative outcome

If the correction turns out to help retrieval but hurt inside a model, that would be the most revealing result of all. It would show that frequency and importance genuinely come apart inside a transformer, for instance because the pages selected most often are attention sinks the model cannot do without.

§12Engineering for the hardware we had

Our nodes have four GPUs with 11 GB each and support neither bf16 nor FlashAttention-2. The full grid takes days to run, so every stage writes a manifest recording its configuration hash, the checksums of its inputs and the code version. Each stage can resume from checkpoints and verifies checksums before reusing anything it has already produced. Three bottlenecks dominated the first full pass, and each needed its own fix.

BottleneckProblemFixEffect
EncodingThe E5 forward pass over MS MARCO was repeated for every basis, seven times over. Run E5 once per batch and apply every basis to the same GPU activations, sharded across four workers. 182 encoding tasks became 57 shared units, and the full corpus encoded in under seven hours.
PostingsPostings were re-transformed for every query batch under each of the eight configurations. Cache the transformed postings on local disk, keyed by the parent artefact, the collection statistics and the switch setting. Loading takes 1.1 s against 7.4 s to rebuild, and all 512 ranking comparisons matched exactly.
ScoringCPU scoring became the last bottleneck. A GPU path that scores 512 queries per pass in fp32, adds products in feature order without fused multiply-add, and breaks ties by document ID. 512 queries went from 24.0 s to 1.8 s, matching the CPU path exactly on 960 comparisons.

Two smaller decisions are worth noting. The encoding batch size stays at 8, because larger batches pushed some quantised assignments outside our numerical tolerance. And exact dense search over 8.8 million passages does not fit on a single 11 GB card, so it runs over shards on the GPU and merges the results from each shard.

why exact matches matter

With a top-k cut-off, even a tiny floating-point difference can swap two documents near the boundary and change nDCG. If the faster GPU path did not reproduce the CPU rankings exactly, we would have no way of telling a genuine effect of a switch from arithmetic noise. That is why the GPU path fixes both the order of additions and the tie-breaking rule.

§15Viva questions

Questions that are likely to come up about the mid submission, each with an answer short enough to give out loud.

  1. Explain the project in two sentences.

    "Retrieval, KV-page selection and MoE routing all take a dot product against non-negative features and keep the top k, and none of them uses the collection-frequency statistics that BM25 is built on. We test when BM25's corrections help across a controlled family of learned vocabularies, try to predict that from label-free statistics, and then carry the predictor into attention and routing."

  2. Why eight configurations and not just BM25 against the inner product?

    "Because BM25 is really three changes bundled together. Switching each one independently gives us a 2³ factorial design, so we can attribute the gain to individual components. That is how we found that saturation does most of the work and IDF the least."

  3. Why divide the gain by the standard deviation over configurations?

    "To make it unitless, so that the same predicted value means the same thing for nDCG, RULER accuracy and MMLU. The drawback, which we found in the results, is that it saturates: when the inner product is the only outlier, the margin is 8/√7, about 3.02, whatever the size of the gain. It is also bounded by 4."

  4. Why is the pooled PQ margin exactly ±2?

    "Pooled PQ has one active code per subspace, all with the same value, so every document has the same length and saturation maps every value to the same constant. Saturation and length therefore only rescale the scores uniformly, and only IDF can change the ranking. Four configurations score Q000 and four score Q111, so the standard deviation is half the gap and the margin is ±2. We got exactly that on all twelve collections where it is defined, which checks the pipeline end to end."

  5. Your predictor failed. Why should we believe anything else?

    "The failure lies in the target, not in the retrieval grid. The raw results stand on their own: 161 of 179 margins are positive, the exact ±2 checks come out right, and the component split has bootstrap intervals behind it. We traced the failure to the saturating margin, kept the frozen predictor as registered for the transfer test, added a majority-sign baseline, and we label any new target as exploratory."

  6. Why is IDF so weak here when CL-SR loses up to 18% without it?

    "At a budget of 128 document features, the top-k truncation has already removed most of the very common features that IDF would down-weight. That is the explanation Han et al. give. The sparsity family varies the budget to test it directly, which is why we moved it forward."

  7. Why does the inner product over token-level SAE codes score so badly?

    "A token-level document is the sum of its tokens' codes, so features that fire on many tokens build up very large values and dominate the dot product. Saturation was designed for exactly that case, and the saturation effect for the token-level SAE is the largest in the table, at 19.3 points."

  8. How do you get df for KV pages and experts, which are not documents?

    "We use selection counts. For a page, it is how many of 64 sampled prefill positions put it in the top-k. For an expert, it is how many tokens chose it across 256 calibration documents. The scores go through a positive map first, a softplus for Quest and the router probabilities for OLMoE, so that saturation is well defined."

  9. Why is nothing significant after Holm correction?

    "With 2,000 bootstrap draws the smallest possible p-value is 1/2001, and Holm multiplies it by 182, so the floor is 0.091. That is a limit on resolution, not evidence that there is no effect, and 134 rows sit at it. We are rerunning with 20,000 draws, which lowers the floor to 0.009."

  10. What is the biggest limitation?

    "Everything comes from one encoder at one budget. That means we cannot fully separate the basis from the encoder, and IDF's small role might change at larger budgets. On top of that, E5 has seen MIRACL training data, so the Indic results are not about unseen languages."
