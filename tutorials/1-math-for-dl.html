Mathematics for Deep Learning: and other abstract nonsense.

Before the transformer, before attention, before any of the machinery the rest of the course spends the semester on, there is a very small pile of math you cannot get away with not knowing. This tutorial is that pile. It walks from what a vector is to why the last layer of a language model is a softmax, and it does so in eighty-two deliberately slow pages. Read it once now. Come back to it whenever a later slide has a matrix in it and you find yourself squinting.

Tutorial 01 82 pages math prerequisites

§0The tutorial at a glance

The deck is organised into four sweeps, tracked by the four dot-rows along the top of every page. Early days is a one-slide confession about prerequisites. Linear algebra takes you from vectors to LoRA in eight pages. Calculus spends the most time in the room, because every hard idea in deep learning is either a derivative or an update built from one, partials, gradients, Jacobians, Hessians, gradient descent, Newton-Raphson, and a hint of the SVD trick that powers modern optimisers. Finally Deep learning assembles all of the above into a parametrised function, argues from the Universal Approximation Theorem that it is enough, then walks the chain rule back through the layers to build backpropagation, SGD, Adam, and eventually softmax over a vocabulary. That last step is where NLP starts.

The subtitle is doing a lot of work. §2Linear algebra: vectors to LoRA in eight pages

Eight pages. The route: definition of a vector space, matrices as maps between them, two families of "nice" matrices (diagonal and orthogonal), eigenvectors as invariant directions, and finally the outer-product / low-rank story that gives us LoRA. If the last page reads like a magic trick, that is the whole point of the six pages leading up to it.

page 03 · section divider

A section divider. Everything from here to page 10 is linear algebra.

Page 04 / 82
page 04 · vectors, spaces, bases

On the slide

Four definitions stacked on top of each other: vector space, basis, linear combination, and span.

What it means

Forget the axioms for a second. A vector space is any collection of things you can add together and scale by numbers without leaving the collection. Arrows in the plane qualify. Polynomials of degree at most 5 qualify. So do functions from ℝ to ℝ. A basis is a smallest set of these things such that every other one is a unique recipe of "add this much of the first, then this much of the second". The recipe itself is called the linear combination; the set of all possible recipes is the span.

✦ the aha

The whole reason we get to talk about "coordinates", v = ( 3 , − 1 , 4 ) , is that a basis exists. The numbers are the recipe. Change the basis, change the recipe, but not the underlying arrow.

Page 05 / 82
page 05 · matrices as maps

On the slide

A matrix A of shape m × n is not a number-grid, it is a linear transformation, a function that eats an n -vector and hands you back an m -vector.

What it means

The most useful thing to know about any matrix is this: its columns tell you where the standard basis vectors go. Column 1 is where e = ( 1 , 0 , … ) lands after the transformation, column 2 where e 2 lands, and so on. Everything else, matrix-vector product, matrix-matrix product, determinant, falls out of this one fact.

Two consequences of linearity worth internalising: grid lines stay straight and parallel after the transformation, and the origin never moves. If either fails, you are not looking at a linear map.

◉ mental model

When you see y = A x , read it as "y is a linear combination of A's columns, weighted by the entries of x." Every matrix product you meet for the rest of the course is a version of this sentence.

Page 06 / 82
page 06 · diagonal & orthogonal

On the slide

Two families of "clean" matrices: diagonal matrices, which stretch each axis independently, and orthogonal matrices, which rotate or reflect but preserve every length and every angle.

What it means

These are the atoms out of which every other linear transformation is built. The Singular Value Decomposition, which will haunt us later, says that any matrix, no matter how ugly, factors into rotate, then scale along the axes, then rotate again. That is A = U Σ V T . When we hit low-rank matrices in two pages, this is why the picture is so clean.

✦ the aha

det ( Q ) = ± 1 is not a formula, it is a geometric fact: orthogonal matrices preserve volume. Positive determinant means a pure rotation, negative means there is a reflection in there too.

Page 07 / 82
page 07 · invariant directions

On the slide

An eigenvector is a non-zero vector v that the matrix A does not rotate, it only stretches or shrinks. The stretch factor λ is the eigenvalue.

What it means

Most vectors, when you multiply them by a matrix, get pushed off in some new direction. Eigenvectors are the exception: they lie along axes the transformation "believes in", and along those axes it behaves like plain multiplication. Finding them reduces to solving a polynomial, the characteristic equation.

A v = λ v ⇔ ( A − λ I ) v = 0
→ where this goes

Eigenvectors of symmetric matrices are orthogonal, and their eigenvalues are the squared singular values of the SVD. So every time later slides say "singular values", they mean "square roots of the eigenvalues of A T A ." Keep this in your back pocket for page 40.

Page 08 / 82
page 08 · outer products & rank

On the slide

Take two vectors u and v . Their outer product u v T is a matrix, but a very poor matrix, in the sense that every column is a scalar multiple of u . It has rank 1. Sum r of these and you get a matrix of rank at most r .

What it means

A dense d × k matrix stores d k numbers. If we know in advance that the matrix has rank r , we can factor it as B A with B tall-and-thin and A short-and-wide, and pay only r ( d + k ) numbers. When r ≪ min ( d , k ) , that is a huge saving. This is the entire reason low-rank adaptation exists.

Page 09 / 82
page 09 · the bottleneck
Page 10 / 82
page 10 · LoRA

On these slides

The problem: a modern language model's weight matrix W 0 is huge, think 4096 × 4096 , and updating it directly means training sixteen million parameters per matrix, per task. The intrinsic rank hypothesis observes empirically that the update Δ W a task needs is nowhere near that rich. It lives in a much smaller subspace.

The idea behind LoRA

Freeze W 0 . Parameterise the update as Δ W = B A with rank r (typically 4, 8, or 16). Train only A and B . Parameter count drops from d · k to r ( d + k ) , which is more than 99% savings for realistic sizes. The theoretical cover is the Eckart-Young theorem: for any matrix, the best rank- r approximation is given by keeping the top r singular components of its SVD. So if the true update was low-rank all along, we lose essentially nothing.

✦ the aha

Every fact about linear algebra you have accumulated across pages 4-8 is deployed on this one page. Vectors and their outer products (page 8) give you the rank-1 pieces; SVD (page 6, one line about "rotate-scale-rotate") tells you those pieces are optimal; the intrinsic-rank observation says nature is being generous. LoRA is not clever, it is just reading linear algebra correctly.

On these slides

A gentle onboarding to the section's central question, how do we differentiate a matrix? Page 12 reminds you what a derivative is at all: the limit of a rise-over-run as the run goes to zero. Page 13 gives the honest, boring answer to the matrix question: if a matrix's entries are all scalar functions a i j ( x ) , then its derivative is just the matrix whose entries are the individual derivatives d a i j d x .

Why start here

The rest of the tutorial is going to bend the definition of "derivative" in progressively weirder directions, with respect to a vector, then with respect to a matrix, then with respect to a whole neural network's parameter tensor Θ . If the very first move (differentiating a matrix of scalar functions elementwise) doesn't sit comfortably, the later moves won't either.

What this build shows

The five slides pose the same puzzle and then pull the rabbit out. If f ( x , y ) = x 2 + y 2 + 2 x y , is d f d x equal to 2 x + 2 y , or does it need a d y / d x correction? The two 12th-grade instincts disagree.

What it means

They disagree because d f / d x is an underspecified question when there are two independent variables. The fix is the partial derivative ∂ f / ∂ x : differentiate with respect to x while pretending every other variable is a constant. For our f , that gives ∂ f / ∂ x = 2 x + 2 y . Cleanly.

◉ mental model

Picture f ( x , y ) as a hilly landscape over the x − y plane. ∂ f / ∂ x is the slope you would feel if you took a step in the pure x direction. ∂ f / ∂ y is the slope in the pure y direction. Each partial ignores the other axis on purpose.

"Dear Lord, it's Physics!": the gradient

Page 19 / 82
page 19 · components
Page 20 / 82
page 20 · the components are partials
Page 21 / 82
page 21 · the gradient

What this build shows

Package all the partial derivatives of a scalar-valued f : ℝ n → ℝ into a single object. The result is a vector.

What it means

That vector is the gradient ∇ f . It has one entry per input coordinate, and each entry is the partial derivative along that coordinate. Geometrically it points uphill in the steepest direction, and its length is the steepness of that steepest ascent.

∇ f = ∂ f ∂ x 1 ∂ f ∂ x 2 ⋯ ∂ f ∂ x n T
✦ the aha

The title's joke is real. Physicists write the same object as ∇ f = ∑ i ∂ f ∂ x i x ^ i , using unit-vector notation. Mathematicians stack the components in a column and call it a day. Same beast either way.

"Corn on the Jacob": the Jacobian

Page 22 / 82
page 22 · the question
Page 23 / 82
page 23 · every-with-every

Page 25 / 82
page 25 · the Jacobian

What this build shows

The gradient handled a scalar output. What if the output is itself a vector? Then a single "derivative" is not enough, you need one number for every combination of output and input coordinate. That is m × n numbers.

What it means

Stack them in a matrix. The Jacobian J has one row per output coordinate, that row is the gradient (transposed) of that output. It has one column per input coordinate. Entry ( i , j ) is ∂ y i / ∂ x j . If you feed the Jacobian a small displacement δ x , it hands you back the best linear estimate of the resulting δ y . The Jacobian is the derivative, generalised.

◉ dimensions cheat sheet

Given f : ℝ n → ℝ m : the Jacobian is m × n . If m = 1 (scalar output), J collapses to ( ∇ f ) T . If n = 1 (scalar input), it collapses to the ordinary derivative vector. Every subsequent Jacobian in this course will fall into one of these two shapes.

"We've had one breakfast, yes…": the Hessian

page 26 · second breakfast

Page 29 / 82
page 29 · the Hessian

What this build shows

Second derivatives. For a scalar-valued f , the first derivative is a vector (the gradient); the derivative of that vector is a matrix, the Hessian H . Entry ( i , j ) is ∂ 2 f / ∂ x i ∂ x j , the sensitivity of the i -th partial to a wiggle in the j -th input.

What it means

Second derivatives tell you about curvature. In 1D, positive f ′′ means the graph is cupping upward, you are near a minimum, not a maximum. In many variables the same information lives in the eigenvalues of H : all-positive means a local min, all-negative means a local max, mixed signs means a saddle. Second derivatives are also the "one other thing" hinted at, they will show up in Newton-Raphson two pages later.

✦ the aha

The convention H = ( J ∇ f ) T is not arbitrary. Because mixed partials commute for nice functions (Clairaut / Schwarz), H is symmetric, a fact that will save your life every time you want to invert one.

Page 30 / 82
page 30 · the hill

On the slide

The core parable of optimisation. You are somewhere on a landscape, blindfolded except for a small patch of ground around your feet. Your job is to get to the lowest point you can. What do you do?

What it means

Feel the slope, take a step downhill, repeat. This is gradient descent. Formally, if the landscape's height is f ( x ) , the direction of fastest ascent is ∇ f , so the direction of fastest descent is − ∇ f . Step size is a hyperparameter you get to pick.

x k + 1 = x k − η · ∇ f ( x k )
⚠ common trap

The phrase "a local minimum will do (for now)" is doing enormous work. Gradient descent finds a minimum, not the minimum. The whole reason deep-learning practitioners obsess over initialisation, learning-rate schedules, and momentum is that the local minimum you land in depends on where you started and how you walked.

Newton-Raphson: walking downhill using curvature

page 37 · multivariate Newton

What this whole build shows

A more principled hill-descent than gradient descent. Instead of just following the slope, we build a quadratic model of the landscape at our current point, using the value, the slope, and the curvature, and jump directly to that model's minimum. Iterate.

What it means

Second-order Taylor says f ( x k + t ) ≈ f ( x k ) + f ′ ( x k ) t + 1 2 f ″ ( x k ) t 2 . Minimise the right-hand side in t : differentiate, set to zero, solve. You get the Newton-Raphson step x k + 1 = x k − f ′ ( x k ) / f ″ ( x k ) .

In many variables the same story becomes x k + 1 = x k − H − 1 · ∇ f , you pre-multiply the gradient by the inverse of the Hessian.

⚠ why nobody uses this at scale

The multivariate update needs H − 1 . For a modern network with N = 10 10 parameters, the Hessian is N × N . Storing it is a nonstarter; inverting it is science fiction. This is exactly why the next few pages are about first-order tricks that approximate curvature without ever writing H down.

page 43 · a surprise tool

What this build shows

A six-slide teaser for something that will land, in full, at the end of §4. The story: modern optimisers need updates that are matrices, and those updates would be more well-behaved if the matrix had all singular values equal to 1, that is, if it were pushed toward an orthogonal matrix (recall page 6). How do you do that on a GPU without ever calling the SVD?

The trick

Odd polynomials, think p ( x ) = 3 x − 3 x 3 + x 5 , have a magical property: p ( U Σ V T ) = U p ( Σ ) V T . Applying the polynomial to the whole matrix is the same as applying it to each singular value in isolation. So if we can find a polynomial p such that iterating p on any real number in ( 0 , ∞ ) converges to 1, then iterating p on any matrix will drive all its singular values to 1, without ever computing the SVD explicitly. Only matrix multiplies, which GPUs adore.

→ payoff at page 75

This is called Newton-Schulz iteration, and it is the beating heart of the Muon optimizer, which shows up at the very end of the deep-learning section. When you get there, remember these six pages, they are the whole reason Muon works at all.

What this build shows

The definition of deep learning, unpacked slowly. The world is full of functions we care about, from images to labels, from English to French, from prompt to next token, and most of them are not linear. So we pick a family of parametrised functions f ^ ( x ; Θ ) , flexible enough to plausibly contain a good approximation of the target f ( x ) , and we search over Θ for the setting that best fits the data.

◉ what Θ actually is

Θ is every tunable number in the network, all the weight matrices A i , all the bias vectors b i , all the layer-norm scale factors, all the embedding entries. For a 7B model that is seven billion numbers. Section §3 is going to earn its rent here.

What this build shows

The geometry of classification. Splitting an m -dimensional input space into n class regions is the same as drawing n − 1 hyperplanes (lines in 2D, planes in 3D, etc). For "nice" data, the first two grids, one straight line does it. Then comes XOR: red at NE and SW, green at NW and SE. No single straight line separates them.

✦ the aha

This "oh no" moment is exactly why the field spent a decade after 1969 in the neural-net winter. Minsky and Papert showed that a single-layer perceptron cannot solve XOR. The fix, hinted at on the very next pages, is composition with a nonlinearity: bend the input space before you try to cut it, and the problem becomes linearly separable in the bent space.

"Ain't linear no mo": why we need nonlinearities

Page 54 / 82
page 54 · composing affine maps
Page 55 / 82
page 55 · still affine
Page 56 / 82
page 56 · add σ, get real

What this build shows

Why simply stacking linear layers is useless, and what fixes it. Composing two affine maps x ↦ A 1 ( A 2 x + b 2 ) + b 1 collapses algebraically to a single affine map A ′ x + b ′ . A hundred layers deep, still one affine map. XOR still unsolvable.

The fix: sandwich a pointwise nonlinearity σ between the layers. Now the composition f 1 ∘ f 2 ∘ f 3 ∘ ⋯ is a genuinely new function that cannot be flattened. This is why every deep network, from a 1990s MLP to a 2026 transformer, has ReLU, GELU, SiLU, or some other pointwise squish between its linear layers.

Page 57 / 82
page 57 · Universal Approximation

On the slide

The Universal Approximation Theorem, stated formally. In plain English: given any continuous function on a bounded region, and any tolerance you like, there exists a two-layer network, one linear layer, one nonlinearity, one more linear layer, that agrees with your target function everywhere in the region to within that tolerance.

◉ what the theorem does and does not promise

Does promise: such a network exists. Does not promise: you can find its weights with gradient descent, or that the required width k is small, or that the network generalises to unseen data. UAT is an existence result, not a training guarantee.

"Begging the question 101": the loss

Page 60 / 82
page 60 · but who picks Θ?

Universal approximation guarantees the right Θ exists. It says nothing about how to find it. That is the question the rest of the section answers.

Page 61 / 82
page 61 · we need a metric
Page 62 / 82
page 62 · cross-entropy loss

What this build shows

Define a loss function L that measures how wrong the model is. The cross-entropy loss, the standard for classification and language modelling:

L ( f ^ ( x ; Θ ) , f ( x ) ) = − ∑ i = 0 n y i log ( y ^ i )

where y i is 1 for the correct class and 0 otherwise, and y ^ i is the model's predicted probability for class i . When the model is confident and right, log ( y ^ ) is close to 0 and the loss is small. When it is confident and wrong, log ( y ^ ) heads to − ∞ and the loss explodes. That asymmetry, being punished more for being confidently wrong than for being timidly wrong, is the whole point.

→ where this goes

Training the model = choosing Θ to minimise L ( Θ ) . That is a many-variable minimisation problem. §3 has already taught you how to attack it, gradient descent needs ∇ Θ L . The next five pages compute that gradient.

What this build shows

How the chain rule, learned in high school, is enough to compute the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter in a network, no matter how deep, no matter how wide.

What it means

For a single-perceptron model f ^ = f ( a x + b ) , the loss depends on a through a chain of three steps: a enters the pre-activation a x + b , which passes through the nonlinearity f to produce f ^ , which enters L . Multiply the three local derivatives together. Same argument works for x and b . And for deeper networks: the ∂ L / ∂ x you just computed is exactly the "loss gradient" the previous layer needs, plug it in as the starting point of a fresh chain-rule expansion.

◉ why "chain rule" and "backprop" are the same slides

Because they are the same idea, phrased twice. Chain rule tells you the mathematical fact; backprop tells you how to compute it efficiently, right-to-left, layer by layer, reusing the downstream gradient as you go. The next five slides restate the same content under the label "Backpropagation" to hammer this home.

Backpropagation: the same idea, with a computer in mind

page 72 · and wider

On the slide

The exact same five slides, one word swapped: "chain rule" → "backpropagation". The deliberate repetition is the pedagogy.

What it means

Backpropagation is what you get when you commit to computing the chain-rule gradients in a specific order: start at the loss (the top of the network), compute ∂ L / ∂ ( output ) , and walk backwards through the layers, at each step turning "gradient with respect to this layer's output" into "gradient with respect to this layer's parameters" and "gradient with respect to this layer's input". The second output becomes the first input for the layer below.

✦ the aha

Nothing is happening here that wasn't already in the chain rule. What backprop adds is dynamic programming: instead of re-deriving the gradient from scratch for every parameter, you compute the shared intermediate quantities once and reuse them. On a modern network with a billion parameters, this is the difference between minutes and centuries.

Optimisers: from SGD to Muon

Page 73 / 82
page 73 · SGD
Page 74 / 82
page 74 · momentum, Adam, AdamW

What this build shows

Everything from §3 crystallises into one line:

θ k + 1 = θ k − η · ∇ θ L

Note the subscript: the gradient is with respect to θ , not x . Data is fixed; parameters are what we tune. The "S" in SGD is stochastic, we approximate the full gradient by averaging over a random mini-batch of examples at each step, which is faster and, surprisingly, often finds better minima than the exact full-batch version.

Why the family grew

Plain SGD is fine but sensitive to the learning rate and slow in valleys where the loss curves gently along one direction and steeply along another. Momentum adds inertia so you barrel through shallow directions. Adam and AdamW track per-parameter statistics of the gradient's mean and variance and adapt the step size accordingly, think of it as approximating the diagonal of the Hessian on the cheap. All of them are trying to buy Newton-Raphson-style curvature information without paying the Newton-Raphson-style price.

Page 75 / 82
page 75 · Newton-Schulz returns
Page 76 / 82
page 76 · the Muon algorithm

What this build shows

The payoff for the six-page teaser on pages 38-43. The Muon optimizer, in five lines:

  1. Compute the usual gradient G t .
  2. Maintain a momentum buffer B t = μ B t − 1 + G t .
  3. Run a Newton-Schulz iteration on B t to orthogonalise it, pushing all its singular values to 1.
  4. Take the resulting matrix O t as the actual update direction.
  5. Update θ t = θ t − 1 − η O t .
✦ the aha

Muon literally uses the fact from §2 that any matrix factors as U Σ V T , plus the fact from §3 that odd polynomials commute with this factorisation, to produce an update that is closer to an orthogonal matrix than plain-gradient methods would give. Every idea in this five-line algorithm was introduced earlier in the tutorial. That is the whole point of ordering the material this way.

"Sike! It's maths all the way down"

page 79 · embeddings, discretised, weak

page 80 · probability vector over V

page 81 · softmax

What this build shows

The resolution to page 78's objection. Instead of the network outputting a discrete token (which breaks differentiability), we have it output a | V | -dimensional vector, one number per token in the vocabulary, that we interpret as a probability distribution over what comes next. Sample from it, or just take the arg-max, whenever you actually need a token.

Enforcing the "probability" part

The output vector has to have non-negative entries that sum to 1. The softmax function is the standard way to enforce both:

softmax ( x ) i = e x i ∑ j = 1 | V | e x j

Exponentiate everything to force positivity; divide by the total to force normalisation. Combined with cross-entropy loss (page 62), this is the entire output stage of essentially every language model built in the last decade.
