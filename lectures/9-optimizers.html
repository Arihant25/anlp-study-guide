Lecture 09 · 29 slides

Optimizers: which way to step, and how far.

Backpropagation hands you a gradient for every weight. The optimizer decides what to do with it. This lecture starts with plain gradient descent and fixes one flaw at a time: momentum for zig-zagging, AdaGrad and RMSProp for giving each weight its own step size, Adam for doing both at once, AdamW for making weight decay behave, and Muon for treating a weight matrix as a matrix instead of a bag of numbers.

Lecture 09 29 slides Manish Shrivastava · Aparajitha Allamraju

§0The deck at a glance

The deck is short and tells one story. Slides 1–3 place the optimizer in the training loop: it is step 4, the weight update. Slides 4–8 cover gradient descent and momentum, which both answer the question which direction should I move? Slide 9 changes the question to how far should I move?, and slides 10–12 answer it with AdaGrad and RMSProp. Slides 13–17 combine both answers into Adam. Slides 18–22 fix how Adam handles regularisation, which gives AdamW, the default optimizer for LLMs for the last several years. Slides 23–28 list what AdamW still gets wrong and introduce Muon. Slide 29 lists further reading.

The same optimizers appear in code in Assignment 2, Part 2, where you implement AdamW and Muon from scratch. Read this page first for the reasoning, then that one for the code.

§1Where the optimizer fits

Every training step does the same four things. The first three are fixed by the model and the loss. The optimizer is the only part of step 4 you get to choose.

Slide 01 / 29
slide 01 · title

On the slide

Lecture 9 of Advanced NLP Monsoon 2026, given on 31 August 2026. The topic is the optimizer: the rule that turns gradients into changes to the weights.

Why it gets a lecture

The earlier lectures built the model: tokens, positions, attention, normalisation, the FFN. None of that matters until the weights are trained, and the optimizer controls the training. Two identical transformers trained with different optimizers can end up at different losses, use different amounts of memory, and need different amounts of compute to get there. At LLM scale these differences cost real money, which is why optimizer research is active again.

Slide 02 / 29
slide 02 · the four steps of a training iteration

On the slide

The training loop in four lines: forward pass, loss, backward pass, update.

In PyTorch terms

The four steps map onto four lines you have written many times:

  1. pred = model(x): forward pass.
  2. loss = loss_fn(pred, y): a single number measuring how wrong the predictions are.
  3. loss.backward(): fills in p.grad for every parameter p. This is the partial derivative of the loss with respect to that parameter.
  4. optimizer.step(): reads each p.grad and changes p.

(Plus optimizer.zero_grad(), because PyTorch adds new gradients to old ones unless you clear them.)

Slide 03 / 29
slide 03 · the optimizer is step 4

On the slide

The same list, with step 4 marked as the optimizer's job.

What to take from it

The gradient tells you how the loss changes if you nudge each weight a tiny bit. It does not tell you how big a step to take, whether to trust this one noisy mini-batch, or whether some weights need bigger steps than others. Every optimizer in this lecture is a different answer to those questions. The gradient is the same input in all of them; only the update rule changes.

✦ Key takeaway

An optimizer is a function from (current weights, current gradient, some memory of past gradients) to new weights. The "memory" part is called the optimizer state, and how big it is turns out to matter a lot (§7).

§2Gradient descent

The simplest possible rule: step against the gradient, by a fixed amount. Slides 4–6 state it, show what the one hyperparameter does, and list where it goes wrong.

Slide 04 / 29
slide 04 · the gradient descent update

On the slide

Gradient descent walks downhill on the loss surface. The update is:

wt+1 = wt − η ∇L(wt)

Reading the equation

  • wt is the vector of all weights at step t.
  • ∇L(wt) is the gradient: a vector, the same size as w, pointing in the direction in which the loss increases fastest.
  • The minus sign turns that into the direction of fastest decrease.
  • η (eta) is the learning rate, a positive number that scales the step.

In practice the gradient is computed on a mini-batch rather than the whole dataset, which makes it a noisy estimate. That version is called stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and the slides use "GD" and "SGD" for the same thing.

Slide 05 / 29
slide 05 · too big a learning rate, too small a learning rate

On the slide

Two parabolas. With a big η, each step jumps past the minimum to the other wall, and the jumps can grow until training diverges. With a small η, every step is safe but there are a great many of them.

A one-line example

Take L(w) = w², so the gradient is 2w and the update is w ← w − η·2w = (1 − 2η)w. Each step multiplies w by (1 − 2η):

  • η = 0.1: factor 0.8. Smooth, steady approach to 0.
  • η = 0.9: factor −0.8. w flips sign every step (the bouncing in the left picture) but still shrinks.
  • η = 1.1: factor −1.2. w flips sign and grows. Divergence.
  • η = 0.001: factor 0.998. Converges, after thousands of steps.
⚠ Watch out

The safe learning rate depends on how steep (curved) the surface is. A real loss surface is steep in some directions and flat in others, so no single η is right for all of them. That one fact drives the rest of the lecture.

Slide 06 / 29
slide 06 · two ways gradient descent wastes steps

On the slide

Two failure modes. (1) On a long gentle slope, GD keeps taking small steps even though every step points the same way. (2) In a narrow ravine, GD bounces between the steep walls and makes little progress along the floor.

Why both happen at once

Picture a long narrow valley: steep across, nearly flat along. The gradient is mostly made of the steep "across" component, so GD spends its steps bouncing wall to wall. If you lower η to stop the bouncing, the steps along the flat floor become tiny. GD has no memory: each step looks only at the current gradient, so it cannot notice that "along the floor" has been the right direction for the last hundred steps. Momentum adds that memory.

§3Momentum

Keep a running average of recent gradients and step along that instead. Directions that agree from step to step add up; directions that flip sign cancel out.

Slide 07 / 29
slide 07 · momentum update

On the slide

vt+1 =βvt +(1−β) ∇L(wt) wt+1 =wt−η vt+1

The picture: plain SGD (blue) settles into the first shallow dip it finds; SGD with momentum (purple) carries enough speed to roll past it into the deeper one.

What v is

v is an exponential moving average (EMA) of past gradients. Each step keeps a fraction β of the old average and mixes in (1 − β) of the new gradient. With the usual β = 0.9, v is roughly the average of the last ~10 gradients (1 / (1 − β) = 10).

How this fixes slide 6

  • The ravine. The "across" component flips sign every step (+, −, +, −), so in the average it mostly cancels. The bouncing shrinks.
  • The gentle slope. The "along" component has the same sign every step, so it does not cancel. Steps in that direction stay full-size and keep coming.
⚠ Two conventions

The slide uses the EMA form with (1 − β). PyTorch's SGD(momentum=0.9) uses v ← βv + g with no (1 − β). The two differ only by a constant factor of 10 at β = 0.9, which the learning rate absorbs. Same algorithm, different η.

Slide 08 / 29
slide 08 · what momentum still gets wrong

On the slide

Momentum still has one global learning rate η. Two problems follow: overshooting (the built-up velocity carries you past the minimum) and the same step size for every parameter, whether it gets a gradient every step or almost never.

Why sparse parameters matter in NLP

Take a word-embedding table. The row for "the" receives a gradient in nearly every batch. The row for "anaphora" receives one only in the rare batch that contains that word. With one global η, the rare row barely moves across all of training, because it gets a handful of small updates while "the" gets thousands. Ideally, a rarely-updated weight would take a bigger step whenever it does get a signal. Momentum cannot do this, because it scales every parameter by the same η.

§4Per-parameter step sizes: AdaGrad and RMSProp

Slide 9 changes the question from which direction to how far. AdaGrad and RMSProp answer it by giving every weight its own learning rate, scaled down for weights that have seen large gradients.

Slide 09 / 29
slide 09 · from "which direction" to "how far"

On the slide

The turning point of the lecture. GD and momentum were about choosing the direction of the step. The next family is about choosing its size, separately for each weight.

Why these are separate questions

A step is a direction times a length. Momentum improved the direction by averaging. But slide 5 showed that the right length depends on how steep the surface is, and slide 8 showed that the right length depends on how often a weight is updated. Both of these differ from weight to weight. So the next idea is to divide each weight's step by something that measures how big its gradients usually are.

Slide 10 / 29
slide 10 · AdaGrad

On the slide

Vt+1 =Vt+ (∇L(Wt))2 Wt+1 =Wt−α ∇L(Wt) Vt+1+ε

(The slide writes the numerator as "∇Wt"; it means the gradient of the loss with respect to W, the same quantity as in the first line.) Everything is element-wise: each weight has its own V.

What each piece does

  • V is a running sum of that weight's squared gradients over all of training.
  • The step is the gradient divided by √V. A weight that has seen big gradients has a big V and gets small steps. A weight that has rarely had a gradient has a small V and gets large steps. That fixes the "anaphora" row from slide 8.
  • ε (around 10−8) keeps you from dividing by zero for a weight that has never received a gradient.
Under the hood

Dividing by √V also makes the step roughly scale-free. If you multiplied the loss by 100, every gradient would be 100× larger, but √V would also be 100× larger, and the step would stay the same. Plain GD would take 100× bigger steps and probably diverge.

Slide 11 / 29
slide 11 · AdaGrad's step size only ever shrinks

On the slide

V is a sum of squares, so it can only grow. The denominator only grows, so the effective learning rate α / √V only shrinks. Eventually it is so small that training stalls.

How fast it shrinks

Suppose a weight gets a gradient of about 1 every step. After t steps, V ≈ t, so its step size is about α / √t. After 10,000 steps that is α/100; after a million it is α/1000. For a convex problem trained once, that decay is fine and is part of why AdaGrad has good theory. For a neural network trained for hundreds of thousands of steps on a loss surface that keeps changing, it means the model stops learning long before it should.

Slide 12 / 29
slide 12 · RMSProp: a moving average instead of a sum

On the slide

Vt+1 =βVt+ (1−β) (∇L(Wt))2

The weight update is the same as AdaGrad's. Only the line for V changes.

Why one change is enough

This is the same EMA trick as momentum, applied to squared gradients instead of gradients. An EMA forgets old values, so V tracks the recent typical size of the gradient instead of the total since step 0. In the example from slide 11 (gradient always about 1), RMSProp's V settles at about 1 and stays there, so the step size stays about α instead of decaying to zero.

The name comes from this: √V is a running root-mean-square of the gradient, and the update divides ("propagates") by it. RMSProp was never formally published; it comes from Geoffrey Hinton's 2012 Coursera lecture slides.

§5Adam

Momentum answers "which direction". RMSProp answers "how far". Adam uses both: the momentum average goes in the numerator, and the RMSProp average goes in the denominator.

Slide 13 / 29
slide 13 · both questions at once

On the slide

Can a single optimizer answer both questions?

The answer, before the slides give it

Yes. Momentum keeps an EMA of gradients (a first moment, roughly the mean) to pick the direction. RMSProp keeps an EMA of squared gradients (a second moment, roughly the mean square) to pick the size. Neither uses the other's statistic, so you can keep both and use both. That optimizer is Adam: Adaptive moment estimation.

Slide 14 / 29
slide 14 · the Adam paper (Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2015)

On the slide

The first page of the Adam paper. The abstract's main claims: it is first-order (gradients only), cheap, needs little memory, is invariant to rescaling the gradient, handles noisy and sparse gradients, and its hyperparameters need little tuning.

Why this paper matters so much

Adam, and its variant AdamW (§6), has been the default optimizer for transformers almost since the transformer was invented. The original 2017 transformer was trained with Adam; so were BERT, GPT-2, GPT-3, LLaMA and most models since. The claim that "the hyperparameters need little tuning" is mostly true: β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999 (often 0.95 for LLMs), and you tune the learning rate.

Slide 15 / 29
slide 15 · Adam vs gradient descent on a surface with a plateau

On the slide

Two paths from the same starting point. Adam (dark blue) reaches the minimum; gradient descent (cyan) is still on its way, slowed down near the flat region marked "plateau".

Why Adam wins on a plateau

On a plateau the gradient is tiny, so GD's step η·g is tiny too. Adam divides by √v, which is also tiny on a plateau, so the ratio stays around 1 and Adam keeps moving at roughly its normal speed. This is the scale-free property from slide 10, and it is Adam's biggest practical advantage: it does not slow to a crawl just because the gradients got small.

Slide 16 / 29
slide 16 · a head-to-head on MNIST (Soydaner, 2020)

On the slide

Ten optimizers, three CNNs, MNIST digit classification. The highlighted rows are the four the lecture has covered so far: SGD, SGD with momentum, RMSProp and Adam.

How to read it

  • All accuracies are between 98.6% and 99.5%. MNIST is easy, so the differences are small.
  • Momentum beats plain SGD on every network, as §3 predicts.
  • AdaGrad is the worst row on all three networks, consistent with slide 11's shrinking step size.
  • Adam is not the winner on any network here. The best accuracies come from AdaDelta (CNN-1) and AdaMax (CNN-2, CNN-3), both members of the same adaptive family.
⚠ Watch out

Don't conclude "Adam is mediocre" from this table. On a small, easy, well-conditioned problem, most reasonable optimizers end up close together. Adam's advantages (robustness to scale, to plateaus and to sparse gradients) matter most on large, badly-conditioned problems like transformer pre-training, where plain SGD often trains much worse.

Slide 17 / 29
slide 17 · the full Adam algorithm

On the slide

Algorithm 1 from the paper on the left, and the same five lines in the lecture's notation on the right:

Mt= β1Mt−1 +(1−β1) ∇L(Wt) Vt= β2Vt−1 +(1−β2) (∇L(Wt))2 M^t= Mt1−β1t , V^t= Vt1−β2t Wt+1= Wt−α M^t V^t+ε

Line by line

  • M is momentum (§3): the EMA of gradients, which picks the direction.
  • V is RMSProp (§4): the EMA of squared gradients, which sets the per-weight scale.
  • The hats are bias correction, the only new idea in Adam. See below.
  • The update is momentum divided by RMS: direction from M̂, size from √V̂.

Why bias correction is needed

M and V start at 0. After one step with gradient g, M1 = (1 − 0.9)g = 0.1g, ten times too small, and V1 = 0.001g², a thousand times too small. Both averages are biased toward zero until enough steps have passed to fill them up. Dividing by 1 − βt undoes this exactly: at t = 1, M̂1 = 0.1g / 0.1 = g and V̂1 = 0.001g² / 0.001 = g². As t grows, βt → 0 and the correction fades away.

✦ Key takeaway

Work out Adam's first step: M̂ / √V̂ = g / |g| = ±1 for every weight (ignoring ε). So the first update moves every single weight by exactly α, whatever the size of its gradient. In general, Adam's step per weight is roughly bounded by α. That is why the learning rate in Adam is roughly "the largest amount any weight moves in one step", and why it is so much easier to tune than SGD's.

§6AdamW: weight decay done right

Regularisation pulls weights toward zero to fight overfitting. With plain SGD there are two equivalent ways to do it. With Adam they stop being equivalent, and the obvious way turns out to be the worse one.

Slide 18 / 29
slide 18 · Adam with L2 regularisation

On the slide

Add an L2 penalty to the loss, 𝓛 = 𝓛error + λ‖w‖². Its gradient adds a term λw to every weight's gradient (strictly 2λw; the 2 is usually folded into λ). That combined gradient then goes into both of Adam's moving averages.

⚠ Notation

Slides 18–22 rename Adam's moments: vt is now the first moment (what slide 17 called M) and st is the second (slide 17's V). Same algorithm, new letters, taken from a different source.

Why this is the obvious thing to do

For plain SGD, adding λw to the gradient gives w ← w − η(g + λw) = (1 − ηλ)w − ηg. The (1 − ηλ) factor shrinks every weight by the same fraction every step: that is literally "weight decay". So for SGD, "L2 penalty in the loss" and "decay the weights directly" are the same thing, and deep-learning libraries implemented Adam's weight_decay argument the first way.

Slide 19 / 29
slide 19 · Adam distorts the decay

On the slide

With Adam, the λw term gets divided by √s like everything else, so different weights are decayed by different amounts.

A worked example

Two weights, both equal to 1, with λ = 0.01. Weight A has had large gradients, so √s ≈ 10. Weight B has had small ones, so √s ≈ 0.1. The decay part of each step is α·λw/√s:

  • A: α · 0.01 / 10 = 0.001α
  • B: α · 0.01 / 0.1 = 0.1α

Weight B is decayed 100× harder than A, and the reason is how big its gradients have been, which has nothing to do with whether it is overfitting. Worse, the weights with large gradients, often the ones you most want to keep in check, are barely regularised at all. Turning λ up does not fix this, because the ratio between A and B stays the same.

Slide 20 / 29
slide 20 · AdamW: decouple the decay

On the slide

Take the penalty out of the loss, so both moments see only the error gradient. Then decay the weights directly, as a separate step. The slide shows the first half; written out, the full update is:

wt+1= wt−α ( v^t s^t+ε +λwt )

The λw term sits outside the fraction, so √ŝ never touches it.

Where the name comes from

The slide title is a pun: Adam with L2 becomes Adam with Weight decay. The method is from Loshchilov & Hutter, "Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization" (ICLR 2019). In PyTorch, torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=…) is still the coupled L2 version and torch.optim.AdamW(weight_decay=…) is the decoupled one, so the class you pick matters.

Slide 21 / 29
slide 21 · why decoupling helps

On the slide

Two benefits: decay is uniform across parameters, and it is no longer distorted by the adaptive learning rates.

Back to the example

With AdamW, weights A and B from slide 19 are both decayed by αλw = 0.01α per step. Every weight loses the same fraction of itself each step, which is what "weight decay" was supposed to mean. A practical bonus: λ and the learning rate now behave more independently, so you can tune one without re-tuning the other.

→ In practice

AdamW is the default for LLM pre-training. A typical recipe: β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.95, weight decay 0.1, with a learning rate that warms up and then decays on a cosine schedule. Biases and normalisation gains are usually left out of the weight decay.

§7Muon: treating a weight matrix as a matrix

Every optimizer so far treats the model as one long list of numbers and updates each number on its own. But most of a transformer's parameters live in 2-D weight matrices, and a matrix has structure that a list does not. Muon uses it.

Slide 23 / 29
slide 23 · three problems with AdamW

On the slide

Three complaints about AdamW.

1 · Memory

Adam stores M and V, each the same shape as the weights. So the optimizer state is twice the size of the model. For a 7B-parameter model in fp32 that is 28 GB of weights plus 56 GB of Adam state, before you count gradients or activations. Optimizer state is often the biggest single item in training memory.

2 · Vector-based

Adam works element by element: each weight's update depends only on that weight's own gradient history. But a weight matrix W in a linear layer is a single object. It maps input vectors to output vectors, and what matters is how it stretches and rotates them as a whole. The struck-out joke on the slide: Adam misses the forest for the trees matrix for the parameters.

3 · Low-rank momentum

Take the momentum buffer for one weight matrix and compute its singular values. In practice a few are large and the rest are close to zero: the matrix is almost low-rank. So each update pushes the weights hard along a handful of directions and barely at all along the rest. Those rare directions may still matter for learning, but they get drowned out.

Slide 24 / 29
slide 24 · no

On the slide

The answer to slide 22: no.

Slide 25 / 29
slide 25 · introducing Muon

On the slide

Two sources share this slide. The footer cites the original Muon, from Keller Jordan and collaborators (2024, a blog post and open-source code). The screenshot is a later paper, Muon is Scalable for LLM Training (Liu et al., Moonshot AI, 2025), which showed that Muon still works at LLM scale.

What the Moonshot paper adds

  • Weight decay. Without it, weights under Muon kept growing over long training runs. They add AdamW-style decoupled decay.
  • Update-scale matching. They rescale Muon's update so its average size per element matches AdamW's. That way, the learning rate and weight decay tuned for AdamW can be reused unchanged.
  • Result. About 2× better compute efficiency than AdamW in their scaling-law experiments, and a 16B-parameter MoE (Moonlight, 3B active) trained on 5.7T tokens with Muon.
Slide 26 / 29
slide 26 · slide 23 again, now as Muon's to-do list

On the slide

Slide 23 repeated. This time, read it as the list of problems Muon is meant to fix:

  • Memory: Muon keeps only a momentum buffer, with no second moment. That is half of Adam's optimizer state.
  • Vector-based: Muon updates each 2-D weight matrix as a whole matrix.
  • Low-rank momentum: Muon's key step (next slide) gives every direction the same weight, so the small directions are no longer drowned out.
Slide 27 / 29
slide 27 · the Muon update

On the slide

Mt= μMt−1 +∇𝓛t (Wt−1) Ot= Newton-Schulz(Mt) Wt= Wt−1 −ηtOt

Lines 1 and 3 are plain SGD with momentum. All the new work is in line 2.

What "orthogonalize" means

Any matrix can be written with its singular value decomposition, M = UΣVᵀ. U and V hold the directions; Σ is diagonal and holds the singular values, one per direction, saying how strongly M pushes along it. Orthogonalizing means replacing every singular value with 1:

M=UΣV⊤ ⟶ O=UV⊤

O keeps every direction M pointed in but gives them all equal strength. This fixes problem 3 from slide 23: in a nearly low-rank momentum matrix, the few dominant directions get scaled down to 1 and the many weak directions get scaled up to 1. Every direction now gets learned.

Why Newton–Schulz instead of SVD

Computing an exact SVD of every weight matrix on every step is too slow on GPUs. Newton–Schulz is an iteration that reaches the same UVᵀ using only matrix multiplications, which GPUs are very fast at. Normalise X0 = M / ‖M‖F (so all singular values are at most 1), then repeat about five times:

Xk+1= aXk+ b(XkXk⊤)Xk + c(XkXk⊤)2Xk

Each step applies the same odd polynomial to every singular value at once. The coefficients (Jordan's are a, b, c = 3.4445, −4.7750, 2.0315) are chosen so that this polynomial pushes any value between 0 and 1 quickly toward 1. Five steps leave them roughly between 0.7 and 1.2, not exactly 1, and in practice that is close enough.

⚠ Watch out

The bullet says "SGD-Nesterov-momentum", but the equations show plain momentum. The reference implementation uses the Nesterov variant: it orthogonalizes g + μM rather than M, which looks one step ahead (see slide 29). Also, Muon is only used for the 2-D hidden weight matrices. Embeddings, the output head, biases and norm gains are still trained with AdamW.

Slide 28 / 29
slide 28 · Muon beats AdamW on devices vs time

On the slide

Each point is a training run that reached the same target loss (1.3 nats on Python code). The y-axis is how many devices it used; the x-axis is how long it took. Lower-left is better: fewer devices, less time.

How to read it

  • The Muon curve lies below and to the left of the AdamW curve. For any number of devices, Muon gets there at least as fast. This is what "expands the Pareto frontier" means.
  • The gap is widest with many devices. To use more devices you raise the batch size, and AdamW gets less out of each example at very large batches. Muon keeps its data efficiency there, so adding devices still saves time.
  • At 8 devices (small batch) the two curves meet. Muon's advantage is mostly a large-batch advantage, which is the regime that LLM pre-training runs in.

§8Further reading

Slide 29 / 29
slide 29 · other optimizers worth knowing

On the slide

Three optimizers left for further reading. One line on each:

  • Nesterov Accelerated Gradient (NAG). Momentum that looks ahead. Instead of taking the gradient at the current point, take it at the point the velocity is about to carry you to, w − ηβv. If you are about to overshoot, that look-ahead gradient already points back, so the overshoot is corrected one step earlier. It is the "Nesterov" in Muon's description (slide 27) and the SGD-Nesterov row of slide 16's table.
  • AdaDelta (Zeiler, 2012). Another fix for AdaGrad's shrinking step size, from the same time as RMSProp. It also replaces the sum of squared gradients with a moving average, and in addition replaces the global learning rate with a running average of past update sizes, so in principle there is no learning rate to tune. It had the best CNN-1 result in slide 16's table.
  • LiMuon. A 2025 variant of Muon designed to be lighter, cutting Muon's memory and compute cost further for large models.

Zoom out. Each optimizer in this lecture fixes one problem with the one before it. Momentum stops gradient descent from zig-zagging. AdaGrad gives each weight its own step size, and RMSProp keeps that step size from decaying to zero. Adam combines momentum and RMSProp and corrects their start-up bias. AdamW moves weight decay out of Adam's adaptive scaling so it applies uniformly. Muon stops treating the parameters as one flat vector and orthogonalizes each matrix's momentum, using half the memory. For an exam, be able to write each update rule and say which problem it fixes.
