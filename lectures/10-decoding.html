Decoding: from a probability distribution to a sentence.

A language model never outputs text. At every step it outputs a probability for each token in the vocabulary, and something else has to pick one. That something is the decoding algorithm. This lecture covers the two families: search methods (greedy, beam search) that hunt for the most probable sequence, and sampling methods (random, top-k, top-p, typical, epsilon, temperature) that draw a sequence at random from a trimmed or reshaped distribution. The central surprise is that the most probable text is often bad text.

Lecture 10 27 slides Manish Shrivastava · Aparajitha

§1From logits to a sentence

The model's last layer produces one score per vocabulary token. Softmax turns the scores into probabilities. Decoding is the rule that picks a token from those probabilities, appends it, and repeats.

Slide 01 / 27
slide 01 · title

On the slide

Lecture 10 of Advanced NLP Monsoon 2026: decoding strategies.

Why it gets a lecture

Everything so far has been about building and training the model. Once it is trained, you still have to get text out of it, and the way you do that changes the output a lot. The same trained model can produce fluent prose, the same sentence repeated forever, or nonsense, depending only on the decoding rule. Decoding is also cheap to change: no retraining is needed, so it is one of the first knobs people turn.

Slide 02 / 27
slide 02 · the output layer: linear, then softmax

On the slide

At each time step the model's final hidden state, a vector of size d, goes through a linear layer that produces |V| numbers, one per vocabulary token. These raw scores are called logits. Softmax turns them into a probability distribution over the next token, here P(· | I saw a cat on a).

The shapes

The linear layer is a matrix of shape d × |V|. For a model with d = 768 and a vocabulary of about 50,000 tokens, every step produces a vector of 50,000 logits. Softmax exponentiates each one and divides by the total, so every probability is positive and they sum to 1. In the picture, tokens like "mat", "sofa" or "table" would get the long bars and most of the other 50,000 tokens would get tiny ones.

Under the hood

The model is autoregressive: whatever token is chosen at step t is appended to the input, and the model runs again to get the distribution for step t + 1. So the choice at each step changes every later distribution. That loop is the reason decoding is a search problem rather than a single argmax.

Slide 03 / 27
slide 03 · the question the lecture answers

On the slide

The question for the rest of the lecture: given a distribution over the vocabulary at each step, how do you produce a sentence?

Two broad answers

You can treat it as search: look for the sequence the model thinks is most probable. Or you can treat it as sampling: draw a sequence at random according to the model's probabilities, so that likely sequences come up often and unlikely ones rarely. Slides 6–14 are about search. Slides 15–24 are about sampling.

Slide 04 / 27
slide 04 · decoding as a function g

On the slide

y^t = g( P(yt| {y<t}) )

Reading the equation

  • {y<t} is everything generated so far (plus the prompt).
  • P(yt | {y<t}) is the whole next-token distribution from slide 2, a vector of |V| probabilities.
  • g is the decoding algorithm. It takes that vector and returns one token, ŷt.

Examples: greedy decoding is g = argmax. Random sampling is "draw one token with these probabilities". Top-k is "zero out all but the k largest, renormalise, then draw".

⚠ Watch out

The equation describes decoders that pick one token at a time from one distribution. Beam search (slide 9) does not fit this form exactly: it keeps several partial sequences alive and chooses among them using their whole history, so its "g" sees more than the current distribution.

Slide 05 / 27
slide 05 · the five strategies

On the slide

The plan: greedy decoding, beam search, random sampling, top-k sampling, top-p sampling. The first two are search methods (deterministic: same prompt, same output). The last three are sampling methods (random: same prompt, different outputs each run). Slides 21–24 add typical sampling, epsilon sampling and temperature.

§2Greedy decoding and exhaustive search

Greedy decoding takes the single best token at every step. It is fast but never reconsiders a choice. Exhaustive search would find the truly best sequence but costs more than any computer could ever pay.

Slide 06 / 27
slide 06 · greedy: argmax at every step

On the slide

Greedy decoding picks the most probable token at each step: ŷt = argmaxw P(yt = w | y<t). The figure shows a recurrent decoder (the boxes of circles are hidden states) producing "he hit me with a pie <END>". Each argmax output, shown by the dotted pink arrows, becomes the next input. Decoding stops when the model outputs the end token.

Why people use it

It is the cheapest decoder there is: one forward pass and one argmax per token. It is also deterministic, which helps in testing and in tasks with one right answer, such as short factual questions or code completion where a mistake costs more than dullness.

Slide 07 / 27
slide 07 · greedy can't take anything back

On the slide

Same figure, with the drawback: once a token is chosen, greedy cannot undo it.

A worked example

Say that at step 1 the model gives P(a) = 0.5, P(b) = 0.4, P(c) = 0.1. After "a" the next distribution is flat, and the best continuation has probability 0.3. After "b" the model is confident, and the best continuation has probability 0.9.

  • Greedy picks "a" (0.5), then the best follow-up (0.3). Sequence probability 0.5 × 0.3 = 0.15.
  • Starting with "b" would have given 0.4 × 0.9 = 0.36, more than twice as probable.

Greedy never sees the second option, because it only compares tokens one step at a time. A locally best choice can lead into a region where every continuation is weak. In translation this shows up as a sentence that starts well and then has to be finished awkwardly, because the first words committed to a structure that does not fit.

Slide 08 / 27
slide 08 · the exact search is exponential

On the slide

The ideal decoder would find the whole sequence y with the highest probability given the input x. By the chain rule of probability, that probability factors into the per-step probabilities the model gives:

P(y|x) = ∏t=1T P(yt| y1,…, yt−1,x)

The slide uses translation as the example: x is the source sentence and y the translation. For a plain language model, drop x or read it as the prompt.

⚠ Two slips on the slide

(1) The slide says find the y that minimizes P(y|x). It should be maximizes. Equivalently, minimise the negative log-probability −log P(y|x), which may be what was meant. (2) It says VT partial translations must be tracked "at each step". At step t there are Vt partial sequences of length t. VT is the number of complete sequences of length T, which is the count that matters for exhaustive search.

How expensive is "far too expensive"?

With a vocabulary of 50,000 and a 20-token output, the number of sequences is 50,00020 ≈ 1094. That is more than the number of atoms in the observable universe (around 1080). Unlike some sequence models, a transformer has no small hidden state that would let dynamic programming merge equivalent prefixes, because each token's distribution depends on the whole prefix. So exact search is out, and every practical decoder is an approximation.

§3Beam search

Beam search sits between greedy (keep 1 candidate) and exhaustive search (keep all of them). It keeps the k best partial sequences at every step.

Slide 09 / 27
slide 09 · keep the k best hypotheses

On the slide

Keep the k highest-scoring partial sequences ("hypotheses"), where k is the beam size, typically 5–10. A hypothesis's score is its log-probability:

score(y1,…,yt) = logPLM(y1,…,yt|x) = ∑i=1t logPLM(yi| y1,…,yi−1,x)

Why logs

The log turns the product of slide 8 into a sum. That avoids numerical underflow (a product of 50 probabilities around 0.1 is 10−50), and it means extending a hypothesis by one token just adds that token's log-probability to the running score. Every score is ≤ 0, and higher (closer to 0) is better.

One step of the algorithm

  1. For each of the k live hypotheses, run the model and get the next-token distribution.
  2. Form all k × |V| one-token extensions, each scored as old score + log P(new token).
  3. Keep the k best of those, across all parents. Discard the rest.

On the example from slide 7 with k = 2: step 1 keeps "a" (log 0.5 = −0.69) and "b" (log 0.4 = −0.92). At step 2 the best extension of "a" scores −0.69 + log 0.3 = −1.90 and the best extension of "b" scores −0.92 + log 0.9 = −1.02. Beam search finds the "b" sequence that greedy missed. The cost is about k times greedy's, instead of exponential.

⚠ Two things to keep straight

(1) The k here is the beam size. The k in top-k sampling (slide 16) is a different thing: how many tokens a sampler may draw from. The slide's phrase "tracking top-k on each step" means the k best hypotheses. (2) Beam search is still approximate. If the best sequence's prefix falls out of the top k at any step, it is gone for good, which is why the slide says "not guaranteed to find an optimal solution". Beam size 1 is exactly greedy.

Slide 10 / 27
slide 10 · when does beam search stop?

On the slide

Greedy decoding stops when it emits <EOS>. In beam search, different hypotheses finish at different steps ("timestamps" on the slide means time steps). A hypothesis that emits <EOS> is complete: set it aside and keep extending the others. Stop when you reach a maximum length T, or when you have collected n completed hypotheses, whichever comes first. Then return the best completed one.

The length problem (not on the slide)

Every extra token adds a negative number to the score, so longer hypotheses score lower just for being longer. If you compare finished hypotheses by raw score, beam search prefers short outputs, sometimes absurdly short ones. The usual fix is length normalisation: compare hypotheses by score divided by length (or by length raised to some power α), which is the average log-probability per token. Most beam-search implementations, including Hugging Face's, have a length-penalty setting for this.

§4Why the most likely text fails

Beam search works well for translation. For open-ended generation, such as continuing a story, it produces dull, repetitive text. Slides 11–14 show the evidence and explain what goes wrong.

Slide 11 / 27
slide 11 · repetition makes itself more likely

On the slide

Two plots of the per-token negative log-likelihood (NLL, −log P, lower means the model finds the token more likely) under two models, labelled "openai" (a transformer LM) and "lstm". On the left, the sentence "I don't know." once. On the right, the same sentence repeated six times. The NLL of each token tends to fall with every repetition. The caption calls this the self-amplification effect.

Why this is bad for beam search

Language models learn that text often repeats itself (names, phrases, list items), so when a phrase is already in the context, the model raises the probability of seeing it again. Each repetition makes the next one more probable. A decoder that chases high probability, like greedy or beam search, will find this loop and get stuck in it, because "I don't know. I don't know. …" really is a high-probability sequence under the model. It is the model plus a probability-maximising decoder that produces the loop.

Slide 12 / 27
slide 12 · a bigger model repeats with more confidence

On the slide

"I'm tired." repeated eleven times. Both models start uncertain, and the NLL falls as the repetition goes on. By the end, the "openai" model (blue) gives the repeated tokens an NLL around 0.2–0.4, which is a probability of roughly 0.7–0.8 per token, and lower than the smaller LSTM (red).

What "scale does not solve it" means

You might hope a bigger, better-trained model would recognise the loop as bad text. The plot shows the opposite: the stronger model is more sure the repetition should continue. This is a property of maximum-likelihood language models in general, so it needs a fix in decoding or in training, not just more parameters.

Slide 13 / 27
slide 13 · fixes for repetition

On the slide

Three kinds of fix.

  • Ban repeated n-grams (decoding hack). While decoding, if a token would complete an n-gram that already appears in the output, set its probability to 0. With n = 3, no three-token phrase can appear twice. It is crude: a story about "the United States" can only say it once. Hugging Face exposes this as no_repeat_ngram_size.
  • Unlikelihood training (Welleck et al., 2020). Add a training loss that pushes down the probability of tokens that already appeared in the context, alongside the usual loss that pushes up the probability of the correct token. The model itself then learns to avoid repeating.
  • Coverage loss (See et al., 2017, from summarisation with pointer-generator networks). Keep a running total of how much attention each source word has already received, and penalise attending to the same words again. This stops a summariser from copying the same sentence twice.

The last line, "using different decoding objectives?", leads into the rest of the lecture: maybe the goal of finding the most probable sequence is itself the problem.

Slide 14 / 27
slide 14 · human text is not high-probability text

On the slide

For 100 tokens of text, the probability the model assigned to each token. Beam-search output (blue) sits near 1 almost all the time. Real human text (orange) swings between near 0 and near 1. This figure is from Holtzman et al. (2020), "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration", the paper that introduced top-p sampling.

The key observation

People do not write by picking the most predictable next word. Most of the informative words in a sentence (a specific name, a number, an unexpected verb) are ones a model could not have predicted with high confidence. Text built only from high-probability tokens is grammatical but says little and repeats itself. So for open-ended generation, the goal should be text that looks like a typical sample from the distribution, not the single most probable sequence.

✦ Key takeaway

The mode of the distribution is not a typical member of it. A 100-token sequence where every token has probability 0.9 has total probability 0.9100 ≈ 3 × 10−5, and almost no real text looks like that. This is the motivation for every sampling method in the next section.

§5Random, top-k and top-p sampling

Instead of searching for the best sequence, draw each token at random from the model's distribution. Plain sampling is too wild, so top-k and top-p cut off the unreliable tail first and sample from what is left.

Slide 15 / 27
slide 15 · plain random sampling

On the slide

For the prompt "He wanted to go to the", the model's next-token distribution has a head (restroom, grocery, store, airport, …) and a long tail. Random sampling draws one token in proportion to its probability: if "restroom" has probability 0.2, it is picked about 20% of the time.

The problem with "you can sample any token"

The slide's second bullet is the flaw as well as the feature. The tail holds tens of thousands of tokens, each very unlikely, but together they can hold a lot of probability mass. If 50,000 junk tokens each have probability 10−6, their total is 5%. Over a 100-token output you would expect about five of them, and one bad token can derail everything after it, because the model then conditions on its own mistake. The model's tail estimates are also the least reliable part of the distribution. Top-k and top-p remove that tail.

⚠ Figure detail

The chart has more bars (around 18) than word labels (10), so the labels do not line up one to one with the bars. Read the labels as examples from the head of the distribution, not as an exact list.

Slide 16 / 27
slide 16 · top-k: sample only from the k most likely

On the slide

Keep only the k most probable tokens, discard the rest, and sample from the survivors. The faded bars and words are the discarded ones.

The precise rule

Let V(k) be the set of the k highest-probability tokens. Then sample from

P~(w) = { P(w) ∑w′∈V(k)P(w′) if w∈V(k) 0otherwise

The division renormalises the survivors so they sum to 1 again. In code, the usual trick is to set every logit outside the top k to −∞ before the softmax, which gives the same result. k = 1 is greedy decoding, and k = |V| is plain random sampling. Values around 40–50 were common in early GPT-2 demos.

Example: probabilities 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1 with k = 2 keep the first two, which together hold 0.7. After renormalising they become 0.4/0.7 ≈ 0.57 and 0.3/0.7 ≈ 0.43.

Slide 17 / 27
slide 17 · choosing k is a trade-off

On the slide

Same figure. Higher k lets more of the tail in: more variety, more risk of a bad token. Lower k keeps only the safest tokens: less risk, but the text drifts back toward greedy's blandness.

Why no single k works

The right k depends on how spread out the distribution is, and that changes from token to token. After "He wanted to go to the" there are dozens of fine next words. After "The capital of France is" there is essentially one. A fixed k treats both the same. The next slide shows both failure cases.

Slide 18 / 27
slide 18 · a fixed k cuts too much or too little

On the slide

Two contexts with very different next-token distributions.

  • Too quickly (orange, flat): after "She said, "I never", many verbs are about equally good: thought, knew, had, saw, did, said, wanted, told, liked, got, and many more. A small k (say 5) throws away perfectly good options like "told" or "liked", making the output less varied than the model intended.
  • Too slowly (purple, peaked): after "I ate the pizza while it was still", "hot" holds nearly all the probability. A k of 10 still lets in "cold", "burning" and "cooking", which are wrong or odd here. After renormalisation each gets a small but real chance of being picked.

The slide does not label the small bar charts drawn above each context. The point to read off is the shape of the next-token distribution beside each one: flat in the first case, peaked in the second.

✦ Key takeaway

The number of tokens worth keeping should depend on the shape of the distribution. Top-p does that by fixing the amount of probability mass to keep, rather than the number of tokens.

Slide 19 / 27
slide 19 · top-p (nucleus) sampling

On the slide

Top-p, also called nucleus sampling (Holtzman et al., 2020), keeps the smallest set of most-probable tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, then samples from that set after renormalising. The number of tokens kept now changes with the distribution.

The precise rule

Sort tokens by probability, highest first. The nucleus V(p) is the smallest prefix of that list with

∑w∈V(p) P(w|y<t) ≥p

Two worked examples with p = 0.9

  • Flat: probabilities 0.5, 0.2, 0.15, 0.1, 0.05. Running totals 0.5, 0.7, 0.85, 0.95. The total first reaches 0.9 at the fourth token, so the nucleus has 4 tokens. Each is divided by 0.95 to renormalise.
  • Peaked: probabilities 0.92, 0.03, 0.02, … The first token alone already holds 0.92 ≥ 0.9, so the nucleus has 1 token and the step is effectively greedy.

That is the "varies the k" in the last bullet: on slide 18, top-p would keep many verbs after "I never" and only "hot" (perhaps with "warm") after "still". Common settings are p = 0.9 to 0.95. p = 1 is plain random sampling.

Slide 20 / 27
slide 20 · the nucleus grows and shrinks with the distribution

On the slide

Three different next-token distributions, Pt1, Pt2 and Pt3. The superscripts just number three different contexts, not powers. The bottom row boxes the nucleus for the same p in each case.

  • Pink (moderate slope): a medium-sized nucleus, about 6 tokens.
  • Blue (flat): it takes many tokens to reach p, so the nucleus is large, about 14 tokens.
  • Green (one dominant token): the nucleus is just the top one or two.

A fixed k would draw the same-sized box around all three. Top-p draws a box of the right size for each, which is exactly the fix for both failures on slide 18.

→ In code

Top-p is implemented by sorting the probabilities, taking a cumulative sum, and masking every token after the point where the sum passes p. Be careful to keep the token that pushes the sum over p, otherwise a very peaked distribution can end up with an empty nucleus. A2 §8.2 walks through filter_top_k and filter_top_p line by line.

§6Beyond top-p: typical and epsilon sampling

Top-k and top-p are both truncation rules: they decide which tokens to throw away before sampling. Slide 21 names two later truncation rules with different ideas of which tokens are worth keeping.

Slide 21 / 27
slide 21 · typical sampling, epsilon and eta sampling

On the slide

Two more methods, and two plots comparing where three truncation rules (top-p, eta, epsilon) cut the same distribution.

Epsilon sampling (Hewitt et al., 2022)

Throw away every token whose probability is below a fixed threshold ε (values around 3 × 10−4 are typical), then renormalise and sample. The idea from the paper "Truncation Sampling as Language Model Desmoothing" is that a trained model spreads a little probability over every token as a kind of smoothing, so very small probabilities are mostly noise, whatever the shape of the rest of the distribution.

Eta sampling (same paper, in the plots)

The plots also show an eta cutoff, which the bullets do not mention. Eta sampling makes the threshold depend on the entropy of the distribution: high-entropy (flat) distributions get a lower threshold, so more tokens survive. As we understand the paper, the threshold is η = min(ε, √ε · e−H), where H is the entropy.

Reading the plots

  • Left, flat distribution (thousands of plausible words). Epsilon's fixed threshold cuts at rank ~650 and throws away many reasonable words. Top-p and eta keep about 2,600–2,750.
  • Right, peaked distribution (one dominant word). Top-p keeps only the top word. Epsilon keeps about 8, because several small tokens are still above its fixed threshold. Eta sits between them.

Each rule fails somewhere: top-p can be too strict on peaked distributions, epsilon too strict on flat ones. Eta is designed to behave sensibly in both.

Typical sampling (Meister et al., 2022)

Remember slide 14: human text is not made of the most probable tokens. Typical sampling keeps the tokens whose surprisal (−log P(w), how surprising the token is) is closest to the distribution's entropy H (the average surprisal). Tokens are ranked by |−log P(w) − H|, and the closest ones are kept until their total probability reaches a threshold, as in top-p. So very surprising tail tokens are dropped, but so can be a token that is far more predictable than average.

⚠ Watch out

The slide says typical sampling "reweights the scores based on the entropy". It is more accurate to say it truncates by entropy: it keeps a set of tokens chosen by comparing each token's surprisal with the entropy, then samples from that set in proportion to the original (renormalised) probabilities. The scores of the kept tokens are not reweighted.

§7Task type and temperature

Whether to search or sample depends on the task. Temperature is a separate knob that reshapes the distribution before any of the rules above are applied.

Slide 22 / 27
slide 22 · a spectrum from closed to open-ended tasks

On the slide

Generation tasks lie on a spectrum. At the closed end, the input almost fixes the output: there are only a few good translations of a sentence. At the open end, the input leaves a great deal free: a story prompt has endless good continuations. In order: machine translation, summarisation, task-driven dialogue, chit-chat dialogue, story generation.

Why this decides the decoder

  • Closed tasks: the most probable output usually is the right one, and there is little room for repetition because the output tracks the input. Beam search works well, and was the standard for machine translation.
  • Open tasks: the most probable output is the bland, repetitive one of §4. Sampling with top-p and a moderate temperature works better.

This also explains an apparent contradiction: beam search was the best decoder for years in MT and still looked broken for story writing. Both observations are correct. They come from opposite ends of this line.

Slide 23 / 27
slide 23 · softmax with temperature

On the slide

Pt(yt=w) = exp(Sw/τ) ∑w′∈V exp(Sw′/τ)
  • Sw is the logit for token w, the raw score from slide 2.
  • τ (tau) is the temperature, a positive number. Every logit is divided by it before the softmax.
  • V here is the vocabulary as a set (on slide 8, V was its size).

At τ = 1 this is the ordinary softmax and nothing changes.

A worked example

Three tokens with logits 2, 1, 0.

  • τ = 1: probabilities 0.67, 0.24, 0.09.
  • τ = 0.5: the logits become 4, 2, 0, and the probabilities 0.87, 0.12, 0.02. The top token gains.
  • τ = 2: the logits become 1, 0.5, 0, and the probabilities 0.51, 0.31, 0.19. The distribution flattens.

Dividing by τ scales the gaps between logits. Softmax only cares about those gaps, so a small τ stretches them (sharper) and a large τ squeezes them (flatter). The ranking of tokens never changes, only how unequal they are.

Slide 24 / 27
slide 24 · high temperature flattens, low temperature sharpens

On the slide

Higher τ: flatter distribution, more diverse output. Lower τ: spikier distribution, less diverse output. ("Lss" on the slide is a typo for "Less".)

The two limits

  • τ → 0: the largest logit dominates completely, so sampling becomes greedy decoding. (Implementations treat τ = 0 as "use argmax", since dividing by zero is not defined.)
  • τ → ∞: all logits divided by a huge number are close to 0, so every token gets nearly the same probability, 1/|V|. The output becomes random tokens.
Under the hood

Temperature is not a decoding algorithm on its own. It reshapes the distribution, and then some other rule picks a token. In practice it is applied first, and top-k or top-p is applied to the reshaped distribution. The order matters for top-p: a higher temperature flattens the distribution, so the same p then keeps more tokens. A common setting for chat models is a temperature somewhere around 0.7–1.0 with top-p around 0.9–0.95, but defaults vary between providers.

§8Re-ranking and takeaways

Any decoder can still produce a bad output. A cheap safeguard is to generate several and pick the best one with a separate scorer.

Slide 25 / 27
slide 25 · what if the output is bad anyway?

On the slide

Sampling is random, so even with good settings some outputs will be poor: off-topic, repetitive or factually wrong. The next slide's answer is to stop relying on a single sample.

Slide 26 / 27
slide 26 · generate many, keep the best

On the slide

Re-ranking in three steps: decode several candidates (by sampling several times, or by keeping all k finished beams), score each with some quality measure, and return the highest-scoring one.

Perplexity as a score, and its trap

Perplexity is the exponential of the average per-token NLL: PPL = exp(−(1/T) Σ log P(yt | y<t)). Lower means the model finds the text more predictable. It is the simplest score because the model already provides it. But slides 11–12 showed that repetitive text gets very low NLL, so ranking by perplexity alone brings back exactly the degenerate outputs that sampling was meant to avoid. That is the warning in the slide's parenthesis.

Better scorers

A re-ranker can be any model that scores a property you care about: style, discourse coherence, whether the output is entailed by (factually consistent with) a source document, logical consistency. Scores from several re-rankers can be combined, for example as a weighted sum. The same pattern underlies "best-of-n" sampling with a learned reward model, which is used widely with modern LLMs.

⚠ Watch out

Re-ranking costs n full generations for one output. It also can only choose among the candidates it is given. If all n are bad, it returns the least bad.

Slide 27 / 27
slide 27 · takeaways

On the slide

Three closing points. Decoding is still an open problem. Each decoding algorithm builds in a preference (a bias) for certain properties of the output. And small changes to decoding, such as top-p, have had a large effect on generation quality.

Connecting it to the examples

  • Beam search builds in a preference for high probability. That gives accuracy on closed tasks and repetition on open ones (slides 11–14).
  • Top-k, top-p, typical and epsilon sampling build in a preference for "plausible but not necessarily most probable", by removing the unreliable tail in different ways (slides 16–21).
  • Temperature sets the balance between safe and diverse (slides 23–24).
  • Re-ranking adds preferences the model's probabilities do not capture at all, such as factuality (slide 26).

Top-p is the clearest case of the third point: a few lines of code, no retraining, and it fixed much of the degeneration that made early neural text generation look so poor.

Zoom out. A language model gives a distribution over the next token, and decoding turns a sequence of those distributions into text. Exact search for the most probable sequence is exponentially expensive, so greedy keeps one candidate and beam search keeps k. Those work well when the input fixes the output, as in translation. In open-ended generation the most probable text is repetitive and unlike human writing, so we sample instead, after cutting off the unreliable tail: top-k keeps a fixed number of tokens, top-p a fixed probability mass, epsilon a fixed minimum probability, and typical sampling the tokens with typical surprisal. Temperature sharpens or flattens the distribution before any of these, and re-ranking chooses the best of several outputs. For an exam, be able to run each rule by hand on a small distribution and say which failure it fixes. The next lecture keeps the same output distribution and asks a different question: how to produce the same tokens faster.
