Logistics: the shape of the semester.

This is the practical map for the semester: what you will study, how the marks work, and when everything is due. Read it once now, then return whenever you need to check a deadline or course rule.

Lecture 01 16 slides Manish Shrivastava · Aparajitha Allamraju

§0The deck at a glance

The deck covers the course rules, syllabus, assessment, seminar option, project expectations, and website. There is no NLP theory here, but these details shape the rest of the semester.

§1The opener

Slide 01 / 16
slide 01 · title

On the slide

The course is M23CS7.501, Advanced Natural Language Processing, taught in Monsoon '26 by Manish Shrivastava and Aparajitha Allamraju at IIIT Hyderabad. First lecture: July 30, 2026.

Why keep this in mind

Here, “advanced” means post-transformer. The course assumes you already know the older NLP toolkit and can follow a paper’s architecture diagram. It moves quickly from that starting point.

Slide 02 / 16
slide 02 · logistics

On the slide

Three ground rules: (1) the course has been revised from earlier offerings, (2) reading is papers, blogs and videos, not a textbook, and (3) deadlines are strict.

What to take from it

NLP changes faster than most textbooks can. The reading list therefore mixes papers, blogs, and videos. The useful skill is learning how to follow a fast-moving field, not memorising one fixed source.

⚠ Watch out

Take the deadline policy literally. Exam and project dates are tied to the institute calendar, so an extension can affect everything that follows.

§2What you'll learn

Slides 3 and 4 outline roughly 22 lectures in six themes. Keep this map handy: it makes it easier to place a new paper or idea in the wider course.

Slide 03 / 16
slide 03 · topics I

On the slide

The first half of the syllabus: 17 of the ~22 lectures.

What to take from it

Nine lectures focus on the transformer: tokenization, position, attention, normalization, layer design, activations, optimizers, mixture-of-experts, and scaling laws. That focus makes sense because later topics either build on transformers or challenge them.

The remaining units cover the surrounding toolkit: evaluation, decoding and generation, and quantization. Together, they answer how to assess, use, and run large models efficiently.

✦ Key takeaway

The weighting is a useful signal: transformers are the course’s foundation, while quantization is introduced as an important practical extension.

Slide 04 / 16
slide 04 · topics II

On the slide

The last third of the semester. Four lectures on alternate architectures (RNN-family revival, State Space Models like Mamba, and diffusion language models), four on reasoning in LLMs (reinforcement learning for NLP; DPO, PPO, GRPO; and reasoning strategies like chain-of-thought), plus a "what's next" wrap-up.

What to take from it

Once transformers became the default, two questions became unavoidable: how can attention handle long contexts affordably, and how do we align large models with human preferences? This part of the course explores both, along with alternatives such as state-space and diffusion models.

→ Keep reading

Lecture 6, More Attention, is the first hint of the O(n²) fix. The State Space Models lecture is the second, more radical, one.

§4Exams & the seminar

Slide 07 / 16
slide 07 · exams

On the slide

Two exams, each worth 10%. Both are scheduled by the institute rather than the instructor.

Why keep this in mind

The past papers from 2023 and 2024 show what to expect: a short, conceptual exam that rewards clear explanations of RNNs, LSTMs, attention, and transformers more than memorised formulas.

Slide 08 / 16
slide 08 · seminar

On the slide

A seminar option. Up to 16 students can pick this; if you do, you present a topic in class and it replaces one of the two exams (worth the same 10%).

What to take from it

The seminar should explain a topic, not retell one paper. Read across a small set of papers, identify the important differences, and present the field to someone new to it.

Assessment covers understanding, coverage, presentation, and your answers during discussion. A polished script helps, but it cannot replace knowing the material well enough to handle questions.

→ Practical note

The form opens after add/drop closes. Watch the course website (slide 16) for it.

§5The project: half your grade

The project accounts for half the course grade, and its early deadlines arrive quickly. This is the part of the deck worth returning to.

Slide 09 / 16
slide 09 · section divider

Slides 9 through 15 cover the project requirements, timeline, and assessment.

Slide 10 / 16
slide 10 · project mechanics

On the slide

You choose the topic and work in teams of three or four. Students without a team can be assigned one, and a rejected proposal may be resubmitted once. The course does not provide GPU compute.

Why the "no compute" line matters

Choose a project that fits the resources you actually have. Pretrained checkpoints, LoRA or QLoRA on small models, synthetic experiments, and careful evaluation studies are all realistic. Pretraining or large-scale ablations are not.

⚠ Watch out

A “small” language model can still need thousands of GPU-hours to pretrain. Check the compute budget before committing to a scope.

Slide 11 / 16
slide 11 · what is NOT a project

On the slide

The bar is publishable. The lecturer explicitly encourages you to submit good work to A* conferences or workshops. And two things are called out as not suitable: plain finetuning / instruction finetuning, and RAG.

Why those two are out

On their own, fine-tuning and RAG usually demonstrate implementation rather than a research contribution. The expectation is that you investigate the model, algorithm, data, or evaluation itself, rather than only wiring existing components together.

Slide 12 / 16
slide 12 · what IS a project

On the slide

Three kinds of project the course explicitly welcomes:

  1. Architecture optimisation & changes. Modify the transformer block, propose a new attention variant, plug in a state-space layer.
  2. Novel evaluation methods. Design a new benchmark, a new metric, or a controlled probe that catches something existing evaluations miss.
  3. Datasets. Curate a new dataset (with the star hinting at "if it's actually novel and non-trivial").

Why keep this in mind

These choices correspond to the three main ways to contribute to a paper: a better model, a better measurement, or better data. Choose the one you genuinely want to spend a semester exploring.

Slide 13 / 16
slide 13 · shopping list of areas

On the slide

A menu of twelve project areas. These aren't project titles; they're regions of the map you can pitch inside.

How to use this list

Some of these labels will be unfamiliar at first. Revisit the list after a few lectures; by then, you will have enough context to see which directions interest you.

→ Related material

Several of these areas map onto later course lectures. Efficient Methods ↔ quantization + Flash Attention (Lecture 6), Reasoning in LLMs ↔ the late-semester block, Diffusion LMs ↔ alternate architectures.

Slide 15 / 16
slide 15 · project grading

On the slide

The 50 project marks split three ways: proposal 10, mid-eval 20, final-eval 20.

Why keep this in mind

The proposal carries the same weight as an assignment, and the mid-evaluation is worth as much as the final one. A clear early plan and steady progress matter; this is not a project to leave until the last week.
