Lecture 01 · 16 slides · July 30, 2026

Logistics — the shape of the semester.

This first deck is not about NLP at all. It is a map: the topics the course will cover, the pieces of work you will be graded on, and the timelines those pieces sit inside. Skim it once now, and come back to it every time you notice a deadline drifting.

Lecture 01 16 slides Manish Shrivastava · Aparajitha Allamraju

§0The deck at a glance

Sixteen slides split into five moves. A title slide, one bullet list of ground rules, two slides listing the course topics, then a run of slides on how the marks are distributed — assignments, exams, a seminar, and a 50% project — and finally a QR code to the course website. There is nothing technical here yet. But everything in the rest of the course lives on the scaffolding this deck sets up.

§1The opener

Slide 01 / 16
slide 01 · title

What this shows

The course is M23CS7.501 — Advanced Natural Language Processing, taught in Monsoon '26 by Manish Shrivastava and Aparajitha Allamraju at IIIT Hyderabad. First lecture: July 30, 2026.

Why it matters

"Advanced" here means post-transformer. The course is not going to spend time re-teaching you what a bag-of-words or a CRF is. It assumes you can read a paper's architecture diagram, and it starts sprinting from there.

Slide 02 / 16
slide 02 · logistics

What this shows

Three ground rules: (1) the course has been revised from earlier offerings, (2) reading is papers, blogs and videos — not a textbook — and (3) deadlines are strict.

The idea behind it

NLP moves too fast for a textbook to keep up. What was state-of-the-art two years ago is now a baseline in someone's ablation table. So the "reading list" is a moving target of arXiv papers, the transformer-circuits blog, Andrej Karpathy on YouTube, and so on. You are being taught to follow the field, not memorise a chapter.

⚠ common trap

"No exceptions for deadline extension" is not lecturer-flavour. It is administrative reality — the mid-sem, end-sem and project deadlines cascade into the institute calendar. Slipping one slips the next.

§2What you'll learn

Slides 3 and 4 are the syllabus. Twenty-two lectures grouped into six buckets. This is worth reading properly — you can use it later to situate whatever paper you are reading against the semester map.

Slide 03 / 16
slide 03 · topics I

What this shows

The first half of the syllabus — 17 of the ~22 lectures.

The idea behind it

The course spends nearly half the semester (9 lectures) on the transformer: tokenization, positional encoding, attention, normalisation, the shape of a layer, activations, optimisers, mixture-of-experts, and scaling laws. This is because every idea in the rest of the course either lives inside a transformer or was built to replace one.

The other buckets are its ecosystem: Evaluation (how you know your model isn't cheating on the metric), Decoding & Generation (beam search all the way through speculative and parallel decoding), and one lecture on Quantization (running these models on a smaller wallet).

✦ the aha

Notice the ratio. Nine transformer lectures, one quantization lecture. If you want to know what the field cares about right now, that's your answer.

Slide 04 / 16
slide 04 · topics II

What this shows

The last third of the semester. Four lectures on alternate architectures (RNN-family revival, State Space Models like Mamba, and diffusion language models), four on reasoning in LLMs (reinforcement learning for NLP; DPO, PPO, GRPO; and reasoning strategies like chain-of-thought), plus a "what's next" wrap-up.

The idea behind it

The mainstream story is: transformer beats RNN, everyone uses transformers, then two things happen — people discover the O(n²) attention cost is painful at long context, and RLHF-style preference optimisation becomes the standard finishing move on any big model. This half of the course is the response to both.

→ where this goes

Lecture 6, More Attention, is the first hint of the O(n²) fix. The State Space Models lecture is the second, more radical, one.

§4Exams & the seminar

Slide 07 / 16
slide 07 · exams

What this shows

Two exams, each worth 10%. Both are scheduled by the institute rather than the instructor.

Why it matters

The mid-sem exam is the same one whose past-year papers appear in the 2023 and 2024 PYQ pages. Looking at those tells you the exam is short (1.5 hours, 30 marks, 3–4 conceptual questions) and rewards clean explanations of RNN/LSTM/attention/transformer machinery — not memorised formulae.

Slide 08 / 16
slide 08 · seminar

What this shows

A seminar option. Up to 16 students can pick this; if you do, you present a topic in class and it replaces one of the two exams (worth the same 10%).

The idea behind it

Notice the wording — "NOT present one research paper but thorough presentation on a topic". The lecturer does not want you to reproduce a single paper's abstract. They want you to synthesise a small area: read three or four papers, see where they disagree, and explain to the room what a newcomer needs to know.

You are judged on four axes: understanding, coverage, presentation, and ability to answer questions. The last one is the trap — you can memorise a script for the first three, but not for questions from the room.

→ practical note

The form opens after add/drop closes. Watch the course website (slide 16) for it.

§5The project — half your grade

Seven slides on the project. If you skim any part of this deck, don't skim this part. It is 50% of your mark and it starts on day one.

Slide 09 / 16
slide 09 · section divider

A section divider. Everything from here to slide 15 is about the project.

Slide 10 / 16
slide 10 · project mechanics

What this shows

The rules of engagement. You propose the topic yourself. Teams are 3 or 4. If you can't find a team, the course will allot you one at random. Rejected proposals get one resubmit. And a small parenthetical: the course cannot give you GPU time.

Why the "no compute" line matters

It rules out a whole class of projects. You cannot pretrain from scratch, you cannot fine-tune a 70B parameter model, you cannot ablate a big architecture at scale. What you can do is work with pretrained checkpoints, use LoRA/QLoRA on a small model, run controlled experiments on synthetic data, or do a clever evaluation study that only needs API calls. Pick a project that fits inside a Colab or a small university GPU allotment.

⚠ common trap

Every year at least one team pitches "we'll pretrain a small language model on Sanskrit". They then discover that a "small" LM still wants several thousand GPU-hours. Scope down early.

Slide 11 / 16
slide 11 · what is NOT a project

What this shows

The bar is publishable. The lecturer explicitly encourages you to submit good work to A* conferences or workshops. And two things are called out as not suitable: plain finetuning / instruction finetuning, and RAG.

Why those two are out

Both are engineering exercises, not research. A finetuning project can be finished in a weekend by copying a Hugging Face notebook; a RAG project is mostly plumbing a vector database to an LLM. Neither teaches you something new about NLP, and neither will get you a paper. The lecturer wants you to touch the model, the algorithm or the evaluation itself — not the connectors around it.

Slide 12 / 16
slide 12 · what IS a project

What this shows

Three kinds of project the course explicitly welcomes:

  1. Architecture optimisation & changes. Modify the transformer block, propose a new attention variant, plug in a state-space layer.
  2. Novel evaluation methods. Design a new benchmark, a new metric, or a controlled probe that catches something existing evaluations miss.
  3. Datasets. Curate a new dataset (with the star hinting at "if it's actually novel and non-trivial").

Why it matters

These map directly onto the three legs a paper stands on: model, metric, data. Pick the one you find most interesting and it will be the leg you sit on for the semester.

Slide 13 / 16
slide 13 · shopping list of areas

What this shows

A menu of twelve project areas. These aren't project titles — they're regions of the map you can pitch inside.

How to use this list

Read it once now. It won't mean much yet: "Interpretability", "Diffusion Language Models", "Retrieval & Extraction" are just names. Come back to it after Lecture 4 or 5 and it will read completely differently — you'll recognise which ones you have opinions about.

→ crossref

Several of these areas map onto later course lectures — Efficient Methods ↔ quantization + Flash Attention (Lecture 6), Reasoning in LLMs ↔ the late-semester block, Diffusion LMs ↔ alternate architectures.

Slide 15 / 16
slide 15 · project grading

What this shows

The 50 project marks split three ways: proposal 10, mid-eval 20, final-eval 20.

Why it matters

The proposal is worth as much as an entire assignment. Treat it seriously — a sloppy proposal signals scope creep to your mentor and costs you both marks and momentum. And the mid-eval is worth as much as the final eval; you cannot save the project for the last week.
