Quantization: fewer bits per weight, nearly the same model.

A 7B-parameter model stored in 16-bit floats needs 14 GB just for its weights. Quantization stores those weights (and sometimes the activations) as 8-bit, 4-bit or even 2-bit integers plus a few scale factors. This lecture starts with how floats are laid out in memory, builds the basic scale-and-round recipe, shows why outliers break it in large LLMs, and then walks through the methods that fix it: LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, GPTQ, AWQ and quantization-aware training.

Lecture 13 33 slides Manish Shrivastava · Aparajitha

§0The deck at a glance

The deck has three numbered parts plus a closing part on training. Slides 1–3 set up the problem: LLMs are too big and too slow for ordinary hardware, and quantization is one of three ways to approximate them more cheaply. Part 1 (slides 4–9) covers how FP32 and FP16 store numbers and defines quantization. Part 2 (slides 10–14) gives the basic recipe, uniform symmetric quantization with a scale factor, shows it in llama.cpp's 4-bit format, and explains why a few huge values (outliers) ruin it. Part 3 (slides 15–30) covers post-training quantization (PTQ) methods for LLMs: LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant deal with outliers in activations, OBQ and GPTQ use second-order information to correct rounding errors, and AWQ protects the weights that meet large activations. Slides 31–33 cover quantization-aware training (QAT), where the model learns to live with rounding, and EfficientQAT, which makes that affordable for LLMs.

Two ideas run through the whole lecture. First, every method still uses the same scale-round-rescale step from slide 11; they differ in how they choose the scale, which numbers share a scale, and what they do about the error that rounding leaves behind. Second, what matters is the error in the layer's output WX, not the error in the weights themselves. That is why so many methods look at the activations X even when they only quantize W.

§1Why make LLMs smaller

Running a large model is limited by memory and by memory bandwidth. Approximating the weight matrices more cheaply attacks both.

Slide 01 / 33
slide 01 · title

On the slide

Lecture 13 of Advanced NLP Monsoon 2026, given on 19 October 2026. The topic is quantization: storing and computing with a model's numbers at lower precision.

Where this fits

Most of the course so far has been about building and training models. This lecture is about running a trained model. Training happens once on a big cluster; inference happens millions of times, often on much smaller hardware. Quantization is the most widely used trick for making inference cheaper. If you have downloaded a "Q4_K_M GGUF" or a "GPTQ 4-bit" model to run on a laptop, you have already used it.

Slide 02 / 33
slide 02 · the three constraints

On the slide

To make LLMs usable on ordinary hardware you want three things at once: low memory use, good output quality, and fast generation.

Putting numbers on "memory footprint"

A model's weight memory is just (number of parameters) × (bytes per parameter). For a 7B model, using 1 GB = 109 bytes:

FormatBits per weightWeight memory, 7B model
FP32327 × 109 × 4 B = 28 GB
FP16 / BF161614 GB
INT887 GB
INT443.5 GB (about 3.9 GB once per-group scales are added, see slide 12)

A consumer GPU with 8 GB of memory cannot hold the FP16 model at all, but holds the INT4 one with room left for the KV cache.

Why memory also means speed

When an LLM generates one token at a time, every generated token needs every weight to be read from GPU memory once. For a single request the arithmetic is small and the reading dominates, so decoding is memory-bandwidth bound. As a rough lower bound, a GPU with 1 TB/s of bandwidth needs about 14 ms to read 14 GB of FP16 weights, so it cannot exceed about 70 tokens per second. At INT4 it reads a quarter as many bytes, so the same bound is about four times higher. Smaller weights are faster weights.

✦ Key takeaway

The three goals pull against each other. Fewer bits help memory and speed and hurt quality. Every method in this lecture is an attempt to push the bits down while keeping quality close to the original.

Slide 03 / 33
slide 03 · three ways to approximate a weight matrix

On the slide

Matrix multiplication is already about as fast as hardware and software can make it. So to go faster, approximate the matrices. Three ways are listed, and the lecture picks the third.

The three options, side by side

Take a weight matrix W of size d × d.

  • Low-rank approximation. Replace W with a product AB, where A is d × r and B is r × d with r much smaller than d. Storage drops from d² to 2dr numbers. This is the idea behind LoRA, though LoRA uses it for fine-tuning updates rather than to compress the base weights.
  • Sparsification (pruning). Set most entries of W to zero and store only the rest. Speed-ups need hardware that can skip zeros, which in practice usually means structured patterns such as 2:4 sparsity.
  • Quantization. Keep every entry and the full shape, but store each entry with fewer bits. Nothing about the model's structure changes, so it works with any architecture.

Quantization is the most popular of the three for LLM inference because it gives a predictable compression ratio (16 → 4 bits is 4×) and can usually be applied to a trained model without any retraining.

§2Floating-point formats

Before shrinking numbers, you need to know how they are stored. Part 1 of the deck opens with the bit layouts of FP32 and FP16 and what is lost when you drop bits.

Slide 04 / 33
slide 04 · part 1 roadmap

On the slide

The plan for Part 1: how FP32 and FP16 represent numbers (slides 5–7), then what quantization is and why you would want it (slides 8–9).

Slide 05 / 33
slide 05 · FP32 vs FP16 bit layout

On the slide

FP32 has 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits and 23 fraction bits. FP16 has 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits and 10 fraction bits. The arrows show that the fraction bits are worth 2−1, 2−2, 2−3, … from left to right.

What each field does

  • Sign: 0 for positive, 1 for negative.
  • Exponent: sets the range, i.e. which power of two the number sits near. More exponent bits mean you can represent much larger and much smaller numbers.
  • Fraction (mantissa): sets the precision, i.e. how finely you can place a number between one power of two and the next. Each extra fraction bit halves the gap between neighbouring representable numbers.

"Sensitivity study by truncation"

The title refers to a simple experiment: drop the low-order fraction bits and see how much the number (and the model) changes. Because bit k of the fraction is worth only 2−k of the leading part, the bits at the right end matter very little. Dropping from 23 fraction bits to 10 changes any number by at most about 2−11 ≈ 0.05% of its size (with rounding). Neural networks are very tolerant of noise that small, which is why FP16 inference is routine.

For example, 1/3 in FP16 is stored as 1365/4096 = 0.333251953…, an error of about 8 × 10−5, or 0.024% of the value.

slide 06 · the same diagram again

On the slide

The same picture as slide 5. In the exported PDF the two slides are identical; in the live lecture this was probably an animation step.

The part the picture leaves out: range

Truncating the fraction costs precision, which is mostly harmless. Cutting the exponent from 8 bits to 5 costs range, which can be harmful. FP32 can hold numbers up to about 3.4 × 1038; FP16 tops out at 65,504, and its smallest normal positive number is about 6 × 10−5. Large activations can overflow to infinity and tiny gradients can underflow to zero. This is why BF16 (bfloat16) exists: it keeps FP32's 8 exponent bits and cuts the fraction to 7 bits. Same range as FP32, less precision than FP16. Most LLM training now uses BF16.

Under the hood

Range versus precision is the same trade-off that integer quantization faces, just handled differently. A float spends bits on an exponent so that every number gets its own scale. An integer format has no exponent, so it needs an external scale factor shared by a group of numbers. Most of this lecture is about choosing that shared scale well.

Slide 07 / 33
slide 07 · encoding −4.40625 in FP32

On the slide

A worked encoding of −4.40625 into FP32, using the formula

x= (−1)S ×F×2E

The encoding, step by step

  1. Sign. The number is negative, so S = 1.
  2. Binary. 4 = 1002. For the fraction, 0.40625 = 0.25 + 0.125 + 0.03125 = 2−2 + 2−3 + 2−5 = 0.011012. So 4.40625 = 100.011012.
  3. Normalise. Move the binary point so there is a single 1 in front: 100.011012 = 1.00011012 × 22. The true exponent is 2.
  4. Bias the exponent. The stored exponent is the true exponent plus a bias of 2k−1 − 1, where k = 8 is the number of exponent bits. Bias = 127, so the stored value is 2 + 127 = 129 = 100000012. The bias lets the exponent field be an unsigned integer while still representing negative true exponents.
  5. Fraction. The leading 1 of 1.0001101 is always there for normal numbers, so it is not stored (the "hidden bit"). The 23 fraction bits are what follows the point, padded with zeros: 0001101 0000000000000000.

That gives the bit pattern on the slide: 1 | 10000001 | 00011010000000000000000.

In FP16 the same number fits exactly too: bias = 24 − 1 = 15, stored exponent 2 + 15 = 17 = 100012, fraction 0001101000. So −4.40625 loses nothing in FP16. Numbers whose binary fraction needs more than 10 bits get rounded.

⚠ Watch out

The formula on the slide is shorthand. Read F as 1.fraction (1 plus the stored fraction bits, here 1.00011012 = 1.1015625), and E as the stored exponent minus the bias (129 − 127 = 2). Check: −1 × 1.1015625 × 22 = −4.40625. If you plug the raw stored bits in directly you get nonsense. Also, the bit positions under the table are labelled 32 and 0, but a 32-bit word has positions 31 down to 0.

§3What quantization is

The definition, and the reason to bother.

Slide 08 / 33
slide 08 · the definition

On the slide

Quantization reduces the numerical precision of a model's weights, its activations, or both, for example from FP32 to INT8. The picture shows a 487 MB FP32 model shrinking to 168 MB at INT8.

Weights versus activations

The phrase "weights and/or activations" hides an important split that comes back all lecture:

  • Weight-only quantization (written W4A16, W8A16, …: 4- or 8-bit weights, 16-bit activations). Weights are stored in low precision and converted back to FP16 just before the matrix multiply. This saves memory and bandwidth, which is what matters for one-request-at-a-time decoding. GPTQ and AWQ are in this family.
  • Weight-and-activation quantization (W8A8). Both inputs to the matrix multiply are integers, so the multiply itself runs on fast integer hardware. This also speeds up compute-bound work such as large batches and long prompts. LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant are in this family.

Weights are fixed after training, so you can study them offline and take your time. Activations change with every input, so their scale has to be guessed from sample data or computed on the fly. That makes them harder, as §6–§7 show.

Slide 09 / 33
slide 09 · the motivation

On the slide

Fewer bits means less memory and less bandwidth, so inference gets faster and bigger models fit on consumer hardware. Example: 487 MB at FP32 becomes 168 MB at INT8.

Checking the example

Going from 32 bits to 8 bits should divide the size by 4: 487 / 4 ≈ 122 MB. The slide says 168 MB, a ratio of about 2.9.

⚠ Watch out

The 487 → 168 MB figure is smaller than the 4× you would expect from 32 → 8 bits. The slide does not say where it comes from. Real conversions rarely hit the ideal ratio because some tensors (often embeddings, normalisation layers or the output head) are left in higher precision, and the scale factors and file metadata take space too. Use "bits per weight × parameters" for the ideal and expect real files to be somewhat larger.

§4Uniform quantization

The basic recipe: pick a scale, divide, round to an integer. To get a real number back, multiply by the scale. Every later method is a refinement of this.

On the slide

Part 2 covers the basic quantization formula and a real 4-bit implementation (slides 11–12), then the outlier problem and two simple fixes, clipping and finer granularity (slides 13–14).

Slide 11 / 33
slide 11 · scale, quantize, dequantize

On the slide

S= max(|α|,|β|) 2b−1−1 Qr=Int (rS) , r^=S·Qr

Reading the symbols

  • r is a real number (a weight or activation). [α, β] is the clipping range: the interval of real values you promise to represent. The simplest choice is α = min and β = max of the tensor.
  • b is the number of bits. A signed b-bit integer, used symmetrically, covers −(2b−1 − 1) … +(2b−1 − 1): −127…127 for INT8, −7…7 for INT4.
  • S is the scale: the real-valued size of one integer step. The largest magnitude in the range maps to the largest integer.
  • Int(·) means round to the nearest integer.
  • Dequantizing multiplies back by S. You do not get r back exactly; you get the nearest grid point r̂. The difference is the quantization error, at most S/2 for any value inside the range.

This scheme is called symmetric or absmax quantization: the grid is centred on 0 and the scale comes from the largest absolute value.

Worked example: absmax at INT8 and INT4

Quantize x = [0.7, −1.3, 0.05, 2.1]. The absolute max is 2.1.

INT8 (S = 2.1/127 ≈ 0.01654)INT4 (S = 2.1/7 = 0.3)

dequantized S·q0.6945, −1.3063, 0.0496, 2.10.6, −1.2, 0.0, 2.1
absolute error0.0055, 0.0063, 0.0004, 00.1, 0.1, 0.05, 0

At INT8 the errors are well under 1%. At INT4 the grid has only 15 points, the step is 0.3, and the small value 0.05 is rounded to exactly zero. Every error stays below S/2 (0.0083 and 0.15). The largest value is always exact, since it defines the scale.

The asymmetric (affine) variant

Symmetric quantization wastes codes when the data is lopsided. Take x = [−0.4, 0.25, 1.05, 2.6], which is mostly positive. With symmetric INT4, S = 2.6/7 ≈ 0.371, and the codes −7 … −2 are never used, because nothing is below −0.4. The affine (asymmetric) scheme maps [min, max] onto the full unsigned range 0 … 2b − 1 and adds an integer zero point z:

S= max−min 2b−1 , z=round(−min/S) q=clamp(round(r/S)+z, 0,2b−1) , r^=S(q−z)

For the example at 4 bits: S = (2.6 − (−0.4)) / 15 = 0.2 and z = round(0.4/0.2) = 2.

The affine grid step is 0.2 instead of 0.371, and the average error roughly halves. The price is one extra stored number (z) per group and a little extra arithmetic. EfficientQAT's formula on slide 32 is exactly this affine scheme.

⚠ Notation

The slide mixes names: it quantizes to Qr but dequantizes from q, and "r = S(q)" means multiplication, S·q, not a function S applied to q. It also says "symmetric" while introducing a range [α, β]: the formula only uses the larger of |α| and |β|, so the range really becomes [−max, max]. Finally, if you clip (slide 14), values outside the range must be clamped to ±(2b−1 − 1) after rounding, a step the slide omits.

Slide 12 / 33
slide 12 · the same formula in llama.cpp's Q4_0

On the slide

C code from llama.cpp (the ggml library) that quantizes one block of weights to 4 bits, next to the formulas from slide 11.

The code, line by line

  • The weights are split into blocks of QK4_0 values (32 in llama.cpp). Each block gets its own scale.
  • The first loop finds amax, the largest |x| in the block.
  • d = amax / ((1 << 3) - 1) is S = amax / (23 − 1) = amax / 7. This is slide 11's formula with b = 4. id is 1/S, so the loop can multiply instead of divide. The scale d is stored with the block.
  • Each value is multiplied by 1/S and rounded, giving an integer in −7 … 7. Adding 8 shifts it to 1 … 15, which fits in an unsigned 4-bit field. The asserts check that it is below 16.
  • pp[l/2] = vi0 | (vi1 << 4) packs two 4-bit values into one byte: the first in the low nibble, the second in the high nibble.

The real cost per weight

A block stores 32 × 4 bits of integers plus one scale. With a 16-bit scale that is 128 + 16 = 144 bits for 32 weights, or 4.5 bits per weight. (With a 32-bit float scale, as const float d suggests for this version of the code, it would be 5 bits per weight.) This is why "4-bit" models are a bit larger than parameters × 0.5 bytes.

Under the hood

Small blocks are the key design choice. One scale per 32 weights means a single large weight can only coarsen the grid for its own 31 neighbours, not the whole matrix. This is the "granularity" fix from slide 14, applied aggressively.

⚠ Watch out

This is an early version of the Q4_0 code. Dividing by 7 only produces 15 of the 16 possible 4-bit codes (code 0 is never used). Later llama.cpp versions changed the details so that all 16 levels are used, and added many other formats (Q4_K, Q5_K, …) with two-level scales. The principle on the slide is unchanged.

§5Outliers, clipping and granularity

The scale is set by the largest value. If one value is huge, every other value gets a coarse grid. This single problem motivates most of the rest of the lecture.

Slide 13 / 33
slide 13 · outliers set the scale for everyone

On the slide

The picture shows a bell-shaped distribution with evenly spaced quantization levels (red lines) covering [α, β]. When the range is set by a few extreme values, the levels are spread wide, and the bulk of the distribution near the centre falls between very few of them.

A worked example

Take x = [0.1, −0.2, 0.15, 0.05, 60] and quantize to INT8 with one absmax scale. S = 60/127 ≈ 0.472. Then 0.1/0.472, −0.2/0.472, 0.15/0.472 and 0.05/0.472 all round to 0. Only the outlier survives. Four of the five values are destroyed, even with 8 bits. If those small values are most of the signal, the layer's output is badly wrong.

Why LLMs have outliers

Weight matrices in trained LLMs are mostly well behaved (roughly bell-shaped around zero). The trouble is mainly in activations: in large transformers, a few hidden dimensions carry values tens of times larger than the rest, and they do so for nearly every token. Slide 16 shows this appearing suddenly at around 6.7B parameters.

Slide 14 / 33
slide 14 · clip the range, or use more scales

On the slide

Two simple fixes. Clipping: choose a range narrower than the true min and max, so the grid is finer where most values are. Finer granularity: instead of one scale for a whole tensor, use one per row or per column.

Clipping: trading two kinds of error

Clipping creates clipping error (values outside the range are clamped to its edge) to reduce rounding error (everything inside gets a finer grid). In the example from slide 13, clipping at ±0.25 gives S = 0.25/127 ≈ 0.00197, and the four small values now get distinct integers (51, −102, 76, 25). But 60 is clamped to 0.25, an error of almost 60. Whether that is a good trade depends on whether the clipped values matter. For weights with a long thin tail it often is; for LLM activation outliers it is not, because those large values carry important information (slide 16). Practical clipping chooses the range that minimises some error measure, such as mean squared error or the change in output, on sample data.

Granularity: who shares a scale

  • Per-tensor: one S for the whole matrix. Cheapest, but one outlier anywhere hurts everything.
  • Per-row / per-column (per-channel): one S per row or column. An outlier only coarsens its own row. If the row [0.1, −0.2, 0.15, 0.05] has its own scale 0.2/127, its values become 64, −127, 95, 32: almost no error.
  • Per-group (per-block): one S per small group of, say, 32 or 128 consecutive values. This is what llama.cpp (slide 12), GPTQ, AWQ and EfficientQAT use ("g128" in later tables).
⚠ Watch out

Finer granularity is not free, and the direction of the outlier matters. For Y = XW, a scale per row of X (per token) and per column of W (per output) can be pulled out of the integer matrix multiply as an outer product. A scale per column of X (per input channel) cannot, because those columns are summed together inside the multiply. LLM activation outliers sit in particular columns of X, which is exactly the awkward direction. §6 and §7 are two different answers to this.

§6LLM.int8(): split off the outliers

Part 3 is about post-training quantization (PTQ): methods that take an already-trained model and quantize it, using at most a small amount of sample data, with no retraining. LLM.int8() is the first: keep the few outlier dimensions in FP16 and do everything else in INT8.

Slide 15 / 33
slide 15 · part 3 roadmap

On the slide

Four PTQ methods. LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant quantize weights and activations to INT8 and deal with activation outliers. GPTQ and AWQ quantize only the weights, usually to 4 bits or fewer. The "pre-requisites" for GPTQ are OBQ and the Cholesky decomposition (slides 23–24).

PTQ versus QAT

PTQ does not change the training procedure. It needs, at most, a small calibration set (a few hundred sample sequences) to measure typical activations. That makes it fast: GPTQ quantizes a 175B model in a few hours. The alternative, quantization-aware training, comes at the end of the deck (§10).

Slide 16 / 33
slide 16 · plain INT8 collapses at 6.7B parameters

On the slide

Mean zero-shot accuracy of OPT models from 125M to 175B parameters. The FP16 baseline (green) and LLM.int8() (blue) lie on top of each other and rise smoothly with size. Plain 8-bit vector-wise quantization (orange) matches them up to 2.7B, then collapses at 6.7B and stays near 0.32 for every larger model.

What "systematic rather than probabilistic" means

In small models, a large activation value shows up now and then, in random places. In models from about 6.7B up, large values appear in the same few hidden dimensions for almost every token and in most layers. They are a feature of the model, not noise. Their magnitudes can be 20× or more larger than typical values (the paper uses magnitude 6 as its outlier threshold, when most activations are near 1).

Plain INT8 quantization of X uses one scale per row (per token). Since almost every token has an outlier in one of these dimensions, almost every row gets a coarse grid, and all the ordinary features in that token are crushed toward zero, as in the slide 13 example. The model breaks.

✦ Key takeaway

You cannot simply clip these outliers either. The paper reports that zeroing out these few outlier dimensions destroys the model's performance, while removing the same number of random dimensions barely matters. The outliers are rare and important at the same time.

Slide 17 / 33
slide 17 · the three-step recipe

On the slide

Split the matrix multiplication XW into two parts: an INT8 part for the regular values and an FP16 part for the outliers, then add the results.

Why splitting is allowed

Matrix multiplication sums over the shared inner dimension: (XW)ik = Σj XijWjk. You can split the index set of j into two groups, O (outlier dimensions) and the rest, and compute each partial sum separately:

XW= ∑j∈O X:,jWj,: + ∑j∉O X:,jWj,:

The first sum uses the outlier columns of X and the matching rows of W, computed in FP16. The second uses everything else, and with the outliers gone the remaining values have a small range and quantize well to INT8. The result is the same product, up to rounding in the INT8 part.

"We quantize individual vectors"

The INT8 part uses vector-wise scales: one per row of X and one per column of W. Each output element (XW)ik is the dot product of row i of X and column k of W, so both scales can be applied after the integer dot product. That is the direction that works with integer matmul (see the gotcha on slide 14).

Slide 18 / 33
slide 18 · the full LLM.int8() pipeline

On the slide

The paper's diagram. Yellow cells are outliers; they sit in whole columns of X. Those columns, and the matching rows of W, go down to the FP16 path. Everything else goes through the INT8 path.

The INT8 path, step by step

  1. Constants. Cx is the absmax of each row of X; Cw is the absmax of each column of W.
  2. Quantize. Multiply each row of X by 127/Cx and each column of W by 127/Cw, then round. This is slide 11's formula with S = C/127, written as multiplication by 1/S.
  3. Integer matmul. INT8 × INT8 products are summed into 32-bit integers (OutI32), so the accumulation does not overflow.
  4. Dequantize. Output element (i, k) was computed from row i scaled by 127/Cx,i and column k scaled by 127/Cw,k. Undo both: multiply by Cx,iCw,k/(127·127). The outer product Cx ⊗ Cw does this for every element at once.

How much is in FP16?

The paper reports that only a small fraction of feature dimensions are outliers (on the order of 0.1% of values), so more than 99.9% of the multiply-adds are in INT8. Weights are stored in INT8, which halves memory compared with FP16, and the zero-shot accuracy matches FP16 at every size on slide 16.

⚠ Watch out

LLM.int8() is mainly a memory method. Finding outliers at run time, splitting the matmul and running two kernels adds overhead, and the original implementation was not faster than plain FP16 matmul for many model sizes. SmoothQuant (next) was designed to get a real speed-up by keeping everything in INT8.

§7SmoothQuant: move the difficulty into the weights

Activations are hard to quantize, weights are easy. Rescale each input channel so some of the activation's range moves into the weight, and both become moderately easy.

Slide 19 / 33
slide 19 · activations are spiky, weights are flat

On the slide

Top row, original model: |X| has one channel spiking to about 10 while the rest are small, so most channels get very few effective quantization levels. |W| is flat and small, easy to quantize. Bottom row, after smoothing: the activation spike is shrunk to about 1, and the weight row for that channel grows a small bump. Both are now easy.

The idea in one sentence

Quantization error depends on the range of each tensor. If you can transfer some range from the tensor that has too much (X) to the one that has room to spare (W) without changing the product XW, you can quantize both to INT8 with per-tensor or per-token scales and never need LLM.int8()'s FP16 side path.

Slide 20 / 33
slide 20 · the smoothing transform

On the slide

Y=XW= (Xdiag(s)−1) (diag(s)W) sj= max(|Xj|)α max(|Wj|)1−α

Reading the equations

  • j indexes input channels: column j of X and row j of W, the two things that get multiplied together inside XW.
  • diag(s)−1 divides column j of X by sj; diag(s) multiplies row j of W by sj. The two cancel, so the product is unchanged before quantization. (The slide writes ≅, but this step is exact; the approximation only enters when you then quantize.)
  • max(|Xj|) is measured on calibration data. α is the migration strength: α = 0 moves nothing, α = 1 moves all the range into the weights, and around 0.5 splits it evenly.

A worked example

Channel j has max |Xj| = 40 (an outlier channel) and max |Wj| = 0.4. With α = 0.5, sj = √40 / √0.4 = √100 = 10. After smoothing, the activation channel's max is 40/10 = 4 and the weight row's max is 0.4 × 10 = 4. With α = 0.5 the two maxima always end up equal, at √(40 × 0.4). A normal channel with max |X| = 1 and max |W| = 0.4 gets s ≈ 1.58: it barely changes.

Why it costs nothing at run time

The division of X by s does not need a separate step. X is the output of a LayerNorm (or a previous linear layer), so you fold 1/sj into that layer's per-channel gain once, offline. The multiplication by s is folded into W before quantizing it. At inference the model just has different weights.

⚠ Notation

The α here (migration strength, a number between 0 and 1) is unrelated to the α on slides 11–14 (the lower end of the clipping range). Same letter, different meaning.

Slide 21 / 33
slide 21 · which operations run in INT8

On the slide

Where SmoothQuant uses INT8 (green) and FP16 (orange) in a transformer block. Every operation with a big matrix multiply is green: the Q, K, V projections, both batched matmuls in attention (QK⊤ and attention × V), the output projection, and both FFN layers. LayerNorm, Softmax and the residual additions stay in FP16.

Why this split

The matmuls are where almost all the compute and weight memory are, so that is where INT8 pays. The orange operations are element-wise or row-wise, cheap, and numerically delicate: Softmax exponentiates its inputs and LayerNorm divides by a standard deviation, so small errors there can be amplified. The residual stream is also kept in FP16 because it accumulates the output of every layer. Compare LLM.int8(), where every matmul also has an FP16 side path. Here there is none.

slide 22 · smoothing works, and keeps working at 175B

On the slide

Top: real activation and weight magnitudes from one layer, before and after smoothing. The tall activation spikes in a few channels are gone; the weights pick up small ridges but stay low. Bottom: accuracy across OPT model sizes. Naive W8A8 and ZeroQuant (another INT8 method) collapse from 6.7B up, the same cliff as slide 16. SmoothQuant-O3 matches FP16 and LLM.int8() all the way to 175B.

What "O3" means

The paper defines several efficiency levels, O1 to O3, that use progressively coarser and cheaper activation scales. O3, the most aggressive, uses per-tensor scales computed offline from calibration data (static quantization). That it still matches FP16 shows how well smoothing removes the outlier problem.

✦ Key takeaway

LLM.int8() and SmoothQuant both reach FP16 accuracy at 175B with 8-bit weights. The difference is cost: LLM.int8() handles outliers at run time with a mixed-precision split, while SmoothQuant removes them offline with a change of variables, so inference is pure INT8 matmul and actually faster.

§8OBQ and GPTQ: fix rounding errors as you go

Rounding each weight to its nearest grid point ("round to nearest", RTN) treats weights independently. But a layer's output depends on weights together. If you round one weight up, you can adjust the others to compensate. OBQ does this optimally and slowly; GPTQ does it almost as well and fast enough for 175B models.

Slide 23 / 33
slide 23 · quantize one weight, correct the rest

On the slide

OBQ quantizes a row of weights one weight at a time. After each one, it updates all the not-yet-quantized weights to cancel the error just introduced.

wq= argminwq (quant(wq)−wq)2 [HF−1]qq δF=− wq−quant(wq) [HF−1]qq · (HF−1):,q H−q−1= (H−1− 1[H−1]qq H:,q−1 Hq,:−1 )−p

The setup

OBQ works one linear layer at a time. For a row w of the weight matrix, and inputs X from calibration data, the error caused by changing the weights by Δ is ‖ΔX‖², which equals ΔXX⊤Δ⊤. So the matrix H = 2XX⊤ (the Hessian of this squared error) tells you how much each weight change, and each pair of changes, costs. Its diagonal says how sensitive each weight is on its own; its off-diagonal entries say which inputs are correlated.

  • F is the set of weights still in full precision. HF is H restricted to them.
  • Line 1 chooses which weight to quantize next: the one whose rounding error, weighted by 1/[HF−1]qq, costs least. That weighted error is proportional to how much the output error grows after the best possible compensation.
  • Line 2 is the compensation δF: how to change all the remaining weights to absorb weight q's rounding error. Its q-th entry moves wq exactly onto quant(wq); the other entries move in proportion to column q of HF−1.
  • Line 3: after quantizing q, it leaves F, so you need the inverse Hessian without row and column q. Re-inverting would cost O(d³). This formula, one step of Gaussian elimination, gets it in O(d²).

A two-weight example

Let H = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] (two positively correlated inputs), so H−1 = (1/3)[[2, −1], [−1, 2]]. The weights are w = [0.6, 0.24] and the grid has step 0.5.

  • Round to nearest: [0.5, 0]. The change is Δ = [−0.1, −0.24], and the cost ΔHΔ⊤ = 0.02 + 0.048 + 0.115 = 0.183.
  • OBQ: quantize w1 = 0.6 → 0.5, error 0.1. Then δ = −(0.1 / (2/3)) · [2/3, −1/3] = [−0.1, +0.05]. So w1 becomes 0.5 and w2 becomes 0.29. Now round w2: 0.29 → 0.5. Final [0.5, 0.5], Δ = [−0.1, 0.26], cost 0.02 − 0.052 + 0.135 = 0.103.

Because the two inputs are positively correlated, lowering w1 can be partly made up for by raising w2. The compensation pushed w2 over the rounding boundary, and the output error dropped by about 44% compared with RTN.

⚠ Watch out

Two slips on the slide. "oneweight" is missing a space. More importantly, the removal formula ends with the subscript −p, but it removes row and column q; it should read −q, matching the left-hand side. (The same typo appears in the GPTQ paper, which is likely where the slide copied it from.)

Slide 24 / 33
slide 24 · Cholesky decomposition

On the slide

A symmetric positive-definite matrix A can be written as A = LL⊤, where L is lower triangular. The slide multiplies out the 3 × 3 case.

How to compute it

Read the multiplied-out matrix column by column and solve for one l at a time:

  • l11 = √a11; then l21 = a21/l11 and l31 = a31/l11.
  • l22 = √(a22 − l21²); then l32 = (a32 − l31l21) / l22.
  • l33 = √(a33 − l31² − l32²).

Small example: A = [[4, 2], [2, 5]] gives l11 = 2, l21 = 2/2 = 1, l22 = √(5 − 1) = 2. Check: LL⊤ = [[4, 2], [2, 1 + 4]] = A.

Why it is a GPTQ prerequisite

Slide 23's removal formula is applied over and over: remove weight 1, then 2, then 3, and so on. Each step is a step of Gaussian elimination on H−1, and doing many of them in floating point lets rounding errors build up until the matrix stops being positive definite, which breaks the algorithm on large models. The GPTQ paper observes that when weights are removed in a fixed order, the rows you need from all these successive matrices are, up to scaling, just the rows of the Cholesky factor of H−1. So you can compute that factor once, with a numerically stable routine, and read the rows off it instead of updating the matrix again and again.

Slide 25 / 33
slide 25 · GPTQ's objective and its two speed-ups

On the slide

argminW^ ‖WX− W^X‖22

Find the quantized matrix Ŵ whose outputs on calibration inputs X are closest to the original layer's outputs. (Here W multiplies from the left, so rows of W are output units and X has one column per calibration token.)

The two speed-ups

  • Arbitrary order. OBQ picks the cheapest weight next, separately for each row, so every row has its own order and its own shrinking HF−1. GPTQ found that for large layers, quantizing columns simply left to right is almost as good. Then every row uses the same order. Since H = 2XX⊤ depends only on the inputs, it is shared by all rows, so the whole column can be quantized and compensated at once with one shared H−1. Cost drops from O(drow · dcol³) to O(max(drow · dcol², dcol³)).
  • Lazy batch updates. Updating the whole remaining matrix after every column does very little arithmetic per memory access, so the GPU waits on memory. GPTQ instead processes a block of B columns (128 by default), updates only within the block as it goes, and applies the accumulated update to the rest of the matrix once per block. The maths is the same; the memory traffic is far lower.

The third ingredient, the Cholesky trick from slide 24, is what keeps this stable at 175B scale.

Slide 26 / 33
slide 26 · the GPTQ algorithm

On the slide

Algorithm 1 from the GPTQ paper. It combines all three ideas: fixed column order, Cholesky rows, and block-wise lazy updates.

Line by line

  • Input. H−1 = (2XX⊤ + λI)−1. The λI term is dampening: a small value added to the diagonal (the paper uses a small fraction of the mean diagonal entry) so the matrix can be inverted even when some inputs are always zero or perfectly correlated.
  • Cholesky. Replace H−1 by its upper-triangular Cholesky factor. From here on, "H−1" in the algorithm means this factor.
  • Outer loop over blocks of B columns. Inner loop over the columns j of the block:
    • quantize column j for all rows at once (RTN onto the grid);
    • compute the scaled error E, which is slide 23's (wq − quant(wq)) / [H−1]qq for every row in parallel;
    • update the remaining columns inside the block using row j of the Cholesky factor. This is slide 23's δF.
  • After the block, apply all B columns' compensations to the rest of the matrix in one matrix multiply (E times a block of the Cholesky factor). This is the lazy batch update, and it is where most of the arithmetic happens, in a GPU-friendly form.
⚠ Notation

The algorithm overwrites H−1 with its Cholesky factor and keeps the same name, so [H−1]jj in the loop is a diagonal entry of the factor, not of the inverse Hessian itself. The column index also changes from q (slide 23) to j.

On the slide

How to read it

  • Table 1. On ResNets, GPTQ is about as accurate as OBQ and the other careful methods (within about one point). The point is not that it wins here, but that it matches them while being fast enough to run on models a thousand times larger.
  • Table 2. The whole of OPT-175B is quantized in 4.2 hours and BLOOM-176B in 3.8 hours, on a single GPU according to the paper. OBQ-style methods could not have been run at this scale at all.
  • 4-bit. At 175B, RTN raises perplexity from 8.34 to 10.54; GPTQ gives 8.37, almost no loss. For BLOOM-176B: 8.11 full, 8.37 RTN, 8.21 GPTQ.
  • 3-bit. RTN falls apart completely (perplexity in the thousands for most OPT sizes). GPTQ stays usable, and at 175B it gives 8.68, still close to full precision.

⚠ Watch out

Some RTN entries are erratic, such as OPT-66B at 4 bits (110) or OPT-1.3B (48.17). They are not misprints in the slide; individual models can have unusually bad outliers that RTN cannot handle. Also note that these results use per-row scales; using small groups (for example g128) improves both RTN and GPTQ.

§9AWQ: protect the weights that meet big activations

AWQ is another weight-only method. Its observation: a weight's importance depends on the size of the activation it multiplies, not on its own size. It protects the important weights by scaling them up before quantizing.

Slide 28 / 33
slide 28 · salience comes from the activations

On the slide

The error from quantizing w in y = Q(w)x is multiplied by x. So the weights that multiply large activations matter most ("salient" weights), and should be protected. AWQ ranks weights by their activation magnitude, measured on calibration data, not by their own magnitude.

Why the activation decides

The output error is (Q(w) − w) · x. The rounding error Q(w) − w is about the same size for every weight in a group, up to half a grid step. So a weight that sees x ≈ 20 contributes 20 times as much output error as one that sees x ≈ 1, whatever the weight's own value. This ties back to the outlier channels in §6: the weights in those input channels are the salient ones.

⚠ Watch out

The first bullet is cut off mid-sentence ("weights that interact with") and the second repeats it. Read them as one sentence: weights that interact with large-magnitude activations are more salient. The last bullet also merges two different things. Keeping the top 1% of salient weights in FP16 is the paper's motivating experiment (panel b on the next slide). AWQ itself does not do that, because mixed precision is slow on hardware. It scales the salient channels instead (panel c).

Slide 29 / 33
slide 29 · scaling a channel shrinks its relative error

On the slide

Q(w)=Δ· Round(wΔ) , Δ=max(|w|)2N−1 Err(Q(w)x)= Δ·RoundErr(wΔ)·x Err(Q(w·s)(xs))= Δ′·RoundErr(wsΔ′) ·x·1s

The argument

Multiply one weight w by s > 1 and divide its input x by the same s. The product wx is unchanged (the same trick as SmoothQuant, used for a different purpose). Now compare the two error formulas:

  • RoundErr is the rounding error measured in grid steps. It is roughly uniform between 0 and 0.5 whatever the value, so on average it is the same in both formulas.
  • Δ′ versus Δ: Δ is set by the largest weight in the group. Scaling up one weight usually does not change the group's maximum, so Δ′ ≈ Δ.
  • That leaves the factor 1/s. The error on that weight's contribution shrinks by a factor of about s.

Intuitively, scaling a weight up stretches it across more grid steps, so the same half-step rounding error is a smaller fraction of it. The x/s division is folded into the previous operation, as in SmoothQuant, so it is free at inference.

The limit

If s is too large, the scaled weight becomes the group's maximum, Δ′ grows, and every other weight in the group gets a coarser grid. So AWQ searches for the scales on calibration data. It sets s = sxα, where sx is the average activation magnitude of each input channel, and grid-searches the single exponent α in [0, 1] to minimise the layer's output error. No backpropagation is needed.

The figure

OPT-6.7B quantized to INT3 with group size 128, perplexity (lower is better): (a) plain RTN 43.2; (b) keep 1% of channels in FP16, chosen by activation size, 13.0, but mixed precision is slow on hardware; (c) AWQ's scaling, also 13.0, with every weight still in INT3.

⚠ Notation

This slide's Δ is slide 11's S under another name, and N is the number of bits (slide 11's b). The denominators also differ: 2N−1 here, as in the AWQ paper, versus 2b−1 − 1 on slide 11. With 2N−1 the largest weight maps to 2N−1, one past the top of a signed range, so it has to be clamped. It is a convention difference, but mixing the two formulas in one calculation gives off-by-one errors.

slide 30 · AWQ needs less, and less specific, calibration data

On the slide

Two experiments on OPT-6.7B at INT3 with group size 128. Left: perplexity as the calibration set grows. Right: what happens when the calibration data comes from a different domain than the test data (PubMed medical abstracts versus Enron emails).

How to read it

  • Calibration size. AWQ is near its best with only 16 sequences. GPTQ needs about 128 to get close, and is still slightly worse at 256.
  • Domain shift. Read each table along the evaluation column. Evaluated on Enron, GPTQ gets 45.52 when calibrated on Enron and 50.41 when calibrated on PubMed: +4.89. AWQ gets 44.57 and 45.07: +0.50. On PubMed, the penalties are +2.33 for GPTQ and +0.60 for AWQ.

Why AWQ is more robust

GPTQ fits many parameters to the calibration data: every weight is adjusted using the exact Hessian XX⊤ of the calibration inputs, so it can overfit to them. AWQ only measures one average activation magnitude per channel and searches a single exponent per layer. Those statistics are similar across domains, so there is much less to overfit.

→ In practice

GPTQ and AWQ are both widely used for 4-bit weight-only LLM checkpoints, and both are supported by common inference engines. They can also be combined with each other or with other methods; the choice is often decided by which kernels your serving stack supports.

§10Quantization-aware training

PTQ adjusts a finished model. QAT goes further: it puts rounding into the forward pass during training, so the weights learn values that survive it. The obstacle is that rounding has no useful gradient.

Slide 31 / 33
slide 31 · fake quantization and the straight-through estimator

On the slide

Left ("Prepared"): during training, the input x and the weight W each pass through a FakeQuant node before the linear layer. Right ("Converted"): after training, the weights are stored as real integers Wq, dequantized for the matmul; the activations are quantized and dequantized on the fly.

Fake quantization

FakeQuant computes Ŵ = S · round(W/S): quantize and immediately dequantize. The result is still a float tensor, so ordinary training code runs unchanged, but it only takes values on the quantization grid. The forward pass therefore sees exactly the errors the deployed model will have, and the loss reflects them. A full-precision copy of W is kept and is what the optimizer updates.

The gradient problem

round(·) is a staircase. Its derivative is 0 on every flat step and undefined at the jumps. So by the chain rule, ∂L/∂W = ∂L/∂Ŵ × 0 = 0 for every weight, and the weights never change.

STE: ∂L∂W ≈ ∂L∂W^

The straight-through estimator pretends rounding is the identity in the backward pass only: whatever gradient arrives at Ŵ is passed straight through to W. Many implementations also set the gradient to zero for values that were clamped outside the range, since nudging them slightly would not change the output.

Why this is reasonable

Over many small steps, the full-precision W drifts. Most steps change nothing in the forward pass, but once W crosses a rounding boundary, Ŵ jumps to the next grid point. The STE gradient says which way to drift, and the loss, which is computed with the rounded weights, says whether the jump helped. The model learns to sit in places where rounding does not hurt.

Slide 32 / 33
slide 32 · EfficientQAT: block-wise, then scales only

On the slide

W^= (clamp( ⌊W/s⌉+z, 0, 2N−1) −z) ·s

This is the affine fake-quantization from the slide 11 worked example: scale by s, round (⌊·⌉ means round to nearest), add the zero point z, clamp to the unsigned N-bit range, then undo the shift and scale. The scale s and zero point z are per group of weights.

The problem and the fix

Plain QAT trains W, s and z for the whole model at once. For an LLM that is as expensive as full fine-tuning (a full-precision copy of every weight, its gradients, and optimizer state) on top of the quantization machinery. EfficientQAT splits it into two cheaper stages:

  1. Block-AP (block-wise training of all parameters). Train one transformer block at a time, with all others frozen. The objective is local: make the quantized block's output match the original block's output on calibration data. Only one block's weights, gradients and optimizer state need to be in memory at a time.
  2. E2E-QP (end-to-end training of quantization parameters). Fix the integer weights Wint (and z), and train only the step sizes s across the whole model with the normal language-model loss. There are very few s values (one per group), so this is cheap, yet it lets the model correct errors that only show up when blocks interact.
Under the hood

Block-AP is like GPTQ and AWQ in matching a local output, but it uses gradient descent with the STE instead of a closed-form or search-based rule, and it trains the weights and the scales together. E2E-QP is close in spirit to parameter-efficient fine-tuning: a tiny set of trainable numbers on top of a frozen, already-quantized model.

On the slide

Average zero-shot accuracy (higher is better) over five common-sense tasks, for Llama-2 7B/13B/70B and Llama-3 8B/70B, at 3-bit and 2-bit weights. The legend on the right explains the column labels.

How to read it

  • 3 bits, Llama-2. All methods are within about 2 points of FP16, and EfficientQAT is closest (64.02 vs 64.86 on 7B). At 70B even RTN loses only about 1.6 points.
  • 3 bits, Llama-3 8B. Much harder. RTN drops from 68.58 to 58.72, almost 10 points; GPTQ gets 60.58, AWQ 64.82, EfficientQAT 67.35. Llama-3 is known to be harder to quantize than Llama-2; one explanation often given is that it was trained on far more tokens, so its weights carry more information per parameter and tolerate rounding less.
  • 2 bits. PTQ methods start to break: OmniQ gets 46.98 on Llama-2 7B (from 64.86). EfficientQAT keeps 59.50 at g128 and 60.14 at g64, and matches or beats the vector-quantization methods AQLM and QuIP# on 7B and 13B. On 70B those two are slightly ahead (69.85 and 70.91 versus 69.48).
⚠ Watch out

The "Group" column does not mean the same thing in every row. For AQLM, "2x8" is a codebook configuration (AQLM stores weights as indices into learned codebooks), not a group size, and QuIP# also uses vector quantization, hence its "-". Smaller groups also cost more bits: g64 stores twice as many scales and zero points as g128, so the two EfficientQAT 2-bit rows are not at exactly the same memory cost.

Zoom out. Quantization replaces each number with an integer and a shared scale, and the whole lecture is about choosing that scale and handling the rounding error. FP16 to INT8 is easy for weights. The trouble starts with activations in models above about 6.7B parameters, where a few hidden dimensions carry huge values. LLM.int8() routes those dimensions through FP16. SmoothQuant rescales channels so the outliers move into the weights and everything runs in INT8. For 4 bits and below, weight-only methods take over: GPTQ uses the Hessian XX⊤ to compensate each rounding error with the weights not yet quantized, and AWQ scales up the weights that meet large activations. When PTQ is not enough, at 2–3 bits or on hard models like Llama-3, quantization-aware training uses the straight-through estimator to let the model adapt to rounding, and EfficientQAT makes that affordable by training block by block and then only the scales. For an exam, be able to quantize a vector by hand with absmax and with a zero point, and say what problem each method solves.
