Before you start

The paper is single-page and text-only. There are no diagrams to complete, no numeric problems, and no code. Every question is an open-ended "describe / explain / compare" prompt. This tells you two things about how the exam is graded.

  1. Marks are given for structure, not for showing intermediate arithmetic. A sub-part worth 5 marks expects roughly five distinct correct claims: a diagram, a defining equation, two How it works sentences, and a "why".
  2. The 3 + 7 split on Q3 is the strongest signal on the paper. The 3-mark half is "describe seq2seq" (a diagram + one paragraph). The 7-mark half is "why attention". Spend your time there.
⚠ Watch out

Because there is nothing to compute, students overwrite. A 5-mark answer that fills two pages of prose is not scoring more than a 5-mark answer that gives one diagram, one equation, and three tight sentences. The rubric caps at 5. Anything past that is time you owed to Q3.

§3Q1 · RNN long dependencies, LSTM vs GRU

Q1 5 + 5 marks RNN LSTM GRU BPTT

The question, verbatim

1. Explain how RNNs enable handling long distance dependencies. Can you compare LSTMs and GRUs at an architectural level? [5 + 5 marks]

How to approach it

The two sub-parts want different things. Sub-part (a) is a How it works story: what does an RNN do that lets information from step 1 reach step 100. This means writing the recurrence, then honestly explaining the failure mode (vanishing gradients) and how gating is the general fix. Sub-part (b) is a side-by-side spec sheet: same structure of "here are the gates, here is the state, here are the equations", once for LSTM and once for GRU. The key's "only arch" note tells you to skip discussion of accuracy, dataset size, or training time; just architecture.

Worked answer

(a) How RNNs enable long-distance dependencies · 5 marks

A vanilla RNN reads a sequence x1, x2, …, xT one token at a time and maintains a hidden state ht that is recurrent: the state at step t is a function of the previous state and the current input:

ht = tanh ( Whh ht−1 + Wxh xt + b )

This is the How it works that enables long-distance dependencies in principle. Because ht is a function of ht−1, and that of ht−2, the hidden state at time 100 has a functional path back to the input at time 1. Information about earlier tokens is carried forward through this shared recurrence, and the same weight matrices are reused at every step, so the model can learn a rule like "if a plural subject appeared, the verb far away must agree" without needing a separate parameter per gap length.

Training uses back-propagation through time (BPTT): the network is unrolled into a T-layer feed-forward net that shares weights across layers, and the gradient of the loss with respect to ht flows backward through every previous step. Every backward step multiplies by the Jacobian ∂ht∂ht−1, which is bounded by the spectral norm of Whh times the derivative of tanh.

This is where the vanishing / exploding gradient problem enters. If that Jacobian has spectral norm less than 1, gradients decay geometrically across steps and long-range signal never reaches the earlier weights; if greater than 1, they explode. So the architectural capacity for long dependencies (a recurrence) is real, but a plain RNN fails to train them in practice.

The fix that makes long dependencies actually learnable is gating. Instead of a single non-linear squash, a gated cell introduces sigmoid gates that decide, per component, how much of the old state to keep and how much of the new input to write. When a gate is near 1 on the "keep" path, the gradient flows across that time step nearly unchanged. This is the additive skip connection through time that gives LSTMs and GRUs their long-range memory.

How it works · in one line

Recurrence gives an RNN the capacity to carry information across time. Gates give it the trainability to do so, by turning the multiplicative gradient path into a nearly additive one on any step where the gate says "keep".

(b) LSTM vs GRU · architectural comparison · 5 marks

Both are gated RNN cells that replace the single tanh update above with a small circuit of gates. They differ in how much state they carry and how many gates they use to route it.

LSTM. Keeps two state vectors: a cell state ct (long-term memory) and a hidden state ht (short-term / output). Three sigmoid gates control it: forget ft, input it, output ot.

ft = σ(Wf[ht−1,xt]+bf) it = σ(Wi[ht−1,xt]+bi) ot = σ(Wo[ht−1,xt]+bo) ct~ = tanh(Wc[ht−1,xt]+bc) ct = ft⊙ct−1 + it⊙ct~ ht = ot⊙tanh(ct)

The forget gate erases parts of the old cell state; the input gate writes a candidate; the output gate decides what of the cell state becomes the exposed hidden state. The cell-state line is the constant error carousel: on any dimension where ft is near 1, the state passes through unchanged and gradients flow back undamped.

GRU. Collapses the cell state and hidden state into a single vector ht, and uses only two gates: update zt and reset rt.

zt = σ(Wz[ht−1,xt]) rt = σ(Wr[ht−1,xt]) ht~ = tanh(W[rt⊙ht−1,xt]) ht = (1−zt)⊙ht−1 + zt⊙ht~

The reset gate decides how much of the past to look at when computing the candidate. The update gate is a single interpolation knob between "keep the old state" and "replace with the new candidate", so it plays the role of the LSTM's forget and input gates fused into one. There is no separate output gate; the hidden state is exposed directly.

Similarities. Both are gated recurrences, both use sigmoid gates and a tanh candidate, both preserve gradient flow through an additive path over time, and in practice both achieve comparable accuracy on most language tasks.

✦ Key takeaway

GRU is often preferred not because it beats LSTM on quality (it usually ties) but because it has roughly 25% fewer parameters and no separate cell state to store. Less memory, less compute per step, and one fewer gate to overfit. The LSTM's extra expressivity (independent forget and input, plus an output-gated view of the memory) pays off mainly on very long sequences and on tasks where the memory and the output need to look different.

What the key says

The key's four bullets (recurrence, backprop, nature of gates, similarity) line up with the solution above. The top-margin table (GRU 2 gates / no external memory / less params / less memory / faster vs LSTM 3 gates / computationally faster) is the marker's rubric for the comparison sub-part, and the side-by-side above hits every one of those rows. The vertical "only arch" reminder is the trap: do not lose time discussing which one wins on which dataset. The question is architectural only.

§4Q2 · ELMo and downstream use

Q2 5 + 5 marks ELMo BiLSTM contextual embeddings

The question, verbatim

2. Describe the ELMO Contextual embedding model. How is it used for downstream tasks? [5 + 5 marks]

How to approach it

Sub-part (a) wants the architecture: a stacked bidirectional LSTM language model, trained on a next-token prediction objective in both directions, whose per-layer hidden states become word representations. Sub-part (b) wants the recipe: one embedding per token is a learned weighted sum of the layer states, where the weights are task-specific parameters that are trained together with a downstream head while the ELMo LM itself is frozen. The key's four bullets are exactly this: stacked BiLSTM, weighted sum of layer embeddings, weights are trained, plug into downstream task.

Worked answer

(a) What ELMo is · 5 marks

ELMo (Embeddings from Language Models, Peters et al. 2018) is a contextual word-representation model: unlike GloVe or word2vec, the vector it assigns to the word bank depends on the sentence it appears in. The vector for bank in "river bank" differs from the vector for bank in "savings bank".

Architecturally, it is a stacked bidirectional LSTM language model trained on a large monolingual corpus:

  1. A character-CNN encodes each input token into a fixed vector xk. This makes ELMo robust to out-of-vocabulary words, so it can embed any string.
  2. A forward LM is a stack of L LSTM layers (typically L=2) trained to predict xk+1 from x1,…,xk.
  3. A backward LM of the same shape is trained to predict xk−1 from the right context.
  4. The two directions are trained jointly, maximising the sum of the forward and backward log-likelihoods.

After training, for a token at position k the model produces 2L+1 representations: the token embedding xk, plus, at every layer j, the concatenation of the forward and backward hidden states:

hk,jLM = [ hk,j→→ ; hk,j←← ]

These per-layer vectors are the raw output of ELMo. The empirical observation Peters et al. make is that different layers capture different linguistic information: the lower layer is closer to syntax (POS, morphology), the higher layer is closer to semantics (word sense, coreference). That is why the final embedding is not just the top layer but a mixture across layers.

How it works · why bidirectional and stacked

Bidirectional gives context from both sides. Without it, the vector for bank in "the bank of the river" would depend only on the words before bank. Stacked lets syntax and semantics live at different depths of the same tower, so the downstream model can choose which depth to lean on.

(b) How it is used for downstream tasks · 5 marks

For a downstream task, say NER or sentiment, ELMo is not fine-tuned. It is used as a frozen feature extractor. For each token, the task model receives one vector produced by taking a learned weighted sum of the ELMo layers, scaled by a per-task gamma:

ELMoktask = γtask ∑ j=0 L sjtask hk,jLM

Here sjtask are softmax-normalised layer weights (they sum to 1) and γtask is a scalar that scales the mixed vector to a magnitude the downstream head is comfortable with. Both are learned during downstream training; they are the only ELMo-side parameters the task ever updates.

The full recipe:

  1. Take the pre-trained ELMo LM and freeze it.
  2. Run the input sentence through it once to get hk,jLM for every token and every layer.
  3. Compute ELMoktask with the current learned sj and γ.
  4. Concatenate that vector with a static input embedding (GloVe / word2vec) if one is used, giving [xk;ELMoktask].
  5. Feed that concatenated vector into the task-specific model (a BiLSTM for NER, a classifier for sentiment, an encoder for QA) and train that model end-to-end. Gradients flow back into sj, γ, and the task model, not into the ELMo LSTMs.

Optionally, ELMo can also be inserted at the output side of the task model instead of the input, and the paper shows that using it at both ends helps some tasks.

✦ Key takeaway

The learned sj is what makes ELMo more than "just the top layer of a BiLSTM". A syntactic task (POS tagging) will learn to lean on the lower LSTM layer; a semantic task (word sense disambiguation) will learn to lean on the upper one. One frozen encoder can serve every downstream task because each task picks its own mixture.

What the key says

The key's four bullets (stacked Bi LSTM, weighted summation of layer embeddings, weights to be computed, downstream task) are exactly the four things above. "Weights to be computed" specifically means the sj and γ are learned, not fixed. A common mistake is to describe ELMo as "just concatenate all the layers" or "just take the top layer", both of which drop that mark.

§5Q3 · Seq2seq with attention

Q3 3 + 7 marks seq2seq encoder-decoder attention Bahdanau

The question, verbatim

3. Describe a sequence-to-sequence machine translation model where decoder pays attention to the encoder. Why is the attention needed? [3 + 7 marks]

How to approach it

The mark split is the most important thing on this question. The 3-mark half is describing an encoder-decoder RNN with attention: a diagram plus one paragraph is enough. The 7-mark half is why attention is needed. Do not answer this with a single sentence. The full argument has three moves: the bottleneck problem in vanilla seq2seq, the "different target words need different source content" observation from the key, and the concrete alignment computation that solves it.

Worked answer

(a) The model · 3 marks

A sequence-to-sequence machine translation model with attention has three pieces:

  1. An encoder BiLSTM reads the source sentence x1,…,xn and produces one hidden state per source position, h1,…,hn. These are the encoder annotations.
  2. A decoder LSTM generates the target sentence y1,…,ym one token at a time. At target step t, its hidden state is st.
  3. An attention block sits between the encoder and the decoder. At every decoder step, it looks over all encoder annotations and produces a context vector ct: a weighted sum of the encoder states, where the weights are computed from how well each source state matches the current decoder state.

The decoder then produces the next target token from its own state and this context vector: p(yt|y<t,x) = softmax(W[st;ct]).

(b) The attention function · part of 7 marks

At decoder step t, attention runs three lines:

et,i = score(st−1,hi) alignment score  αt,i = exp(et,i)∑j=1nexp(et,j) attention weight  ct = ∑i=1n αt,i hi context vector 

The score function is the "attention func" the key wants defined. Two standard choices:

Additive (Bahdanau, 2015). A tiny feed-forward net:

score(st−1,hi) = vT tanh ( Wsst−1 + Whhi )

Multiplicative / dot (Luong, 2015). Cheaper: just a dot product, optionally with a learned matrix in the middle:

score(st,hi) = stT W hi

Once ct is computed, the decoder uses both its own state and the context to predict the next word. In Bahdanau's formulation the context is fed into the decoder RNN update; in Luong's it is concatenated with the decoder state after the update. Either way, the decoder step becomes conditioned on a source view that changes at every target position.

(c) Why attention is needed · the rest of 7 marks

The bottleneck problem. A vanilla seq2seq encoder-decoder (Sutskever et al. 2014) reads the whole source sentence and summarises it into a single fixed-length vector (the final encoder hidden state) and hands that one vector to the decoder as its only source-side input. The decoder must generate the entire target sentence from this single vector. Empirically this collapses on long sentences: translation quality drops sharply once the source is longer than roughly 20 to 30 tokens, because a fixed-size vector cannot carry all of the source content.

Different targets need different source content. This is the key's core observation. Consider translating "the red car is fast" into French, "la voiture rouge est rapide". When the decoder is producing voiture, it needs to look at car. When it is producing rouge, it needs to look at red. A single fixed source vector cannot serve both; it either averages the two signals or forgets one. What the decoder actually needs is a different source view at each target step. That is exactly what attention gives: a fresh ct per step, computed by softly pointing at the source tokens that matter for the current output.

Attention lifts the bottleneck by letting the decoder look back. The encoder no longer has to fit the whole source into one vector, because every one of its per-position states hi is preserved and reachable. At each decoder step, the softmax over alignment scores acts as a soft pointer: it selects (or blends) the source positions relevant to what the decoder is about to output. In effect, the decoder can "look back" at any encoder state at any time, cost-free at inference.

The alignment side-effect. The attention weights αt,i also give a free, interpretable soft alignment between source and target words, which was previously a separate step in phrase-based statistical MT systems. Attention absorbs alignment into the model.

How it works · one-line summary of why

Vanilla seq2seq forces one source vector to serve every target step. Attention gives every target step its own source vector, computed by a soft, differentiable lookup over the encoder's per-position states.

✦ Key takeaway

Attention is more than an accuracy fix for long sentences. It is the observation that "which piece of the input do I need right now" is itself something the model can learn. Once you can learn a soft lookup over encoder states, you no longer need recurrence to carry information across positions; you can just attend directly. That realisation is what leads, three years later, to the transformer.

What the key says

The key's two bullets are (i) describe a RNN/LSTM with attention "func" defined, hit in the alignment score above, and (ii) different target words need different content words for translation, hit in the "why" argument. The mark split (3 + 7) is worth remembering next year: describing the model is cheap, defending attention is where the marks live.
