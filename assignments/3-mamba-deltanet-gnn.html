Assignment 03 · due 13 Nov 2026

Mamba, DeltaNet and GNNs — concepts first, then every line.

This page explains how Assignment 3 was done, in the order it was done. Part A builds Mamba, DeltaNet and a hybrid of the two and trains them to decrypt a cipher, which turns out to be unbreakable. Part B classifies fake news with four graph neural networks written from scratch. Each idea is explained before the code that implements it, and the results come with the reasons behind them.

Assignment 03 Mamba · DeltaNet · hybrid GCN · GraphSAGE · GAT · Bi-GCN UPFD GossipCop

§0What the assignment asks, in one screen

Assignment 3 has two unrelated halves. Part A is about sequence models that are not Transformers: you build Mamba and DeltaNet from their papers, plus a hybrid of the two, and race them against a Transformer on a decryption task. Part B is about graph neural networks: you decide whether a news article is fake by looking at how it spread on Twitter.

the deliverables

Part A. Four models (Transformer, Mamba, DeltaNet, Mamba–DeltaNet hybrid) trained on cipher.jsonl → cleaned.jsonl. Report character accuracy, word accuracy, precision, recall, F1, Levenshtein distance, parameter count and FLOPs. Weights live on Hugging Face and bash scripts/part1/test_assn.sh --heldout must pull them and decrypt a hidden file. The hybrid's design must be written in docs/DESIGN.md and pushed before the hybrid is coded.

Part B. On the GossipCop part of UPFD, train a root-only MLP, GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT and Bi-GCN with BERT features (5 seeds each), then GraphSAGE with each of the four feature types (5 seeds each). Report mean ± std of accuracy, precision, recall and F1 using the best-validation-F1 epoch.

Rules that shape the code. No comments or docstrings in code (decisions go in CHOICES.md), models from scratch (no mamba_ssm), uv for the environment, inference within 10 GB of VRAM.

§1Look at the data before building anything

The most important step of Part A happens before any model exists. If you know what the cipher does, you know what the model has to learn. If the cipher turns out to be impossible to learn, you know what accuracy to expect and you can stop chasing it.

§1.1 · What a sample looks like

cleaned: "valkyria chronicles iii"          23 characters, only a-z and space
cipher:  "suD6OZe=Q04fY7Ty6 oeebip?"        25 characters, 81 different symbols

The plaintext is lower-cased Wikipedia text (WikiText-103) with only a–z and space. The ciphertext uses 81 symbols: space, upper- and lower-case letters, digits, and 18 punctuation marks. The ciphertext is usually a few characters longer than the plaintext.

§1.2 · The punctuation marks are padding

Split the 81 symbols into two groups: the 63 "core" symbols (space, letters, digits) and the 18 punctuation marks !'+,-./:;<=>?[]_{}. Delete the punctuation from every ciphertext and compare lengths:

Check (200k training samples)Result
core-symbol length == plaintext length100% of samples
fraction of ciphertext that is punctuationabout 13%, and it grows with length
how often a punctuation mark sits next to a plaintext space16.97%, while spaces are 16.95% of all characters

The first row says each core symbol corresponds to exactly one plaintext character, in order. The last row says the punctuation is dropped in at random places: it is no more likely to sit at a word boundary than anywhere else. In cryptography these are called nulls, symbols that mean nothing and are there only to confuse. So the first preprocessing step is to delete them. After that, cipher position i lines up with plaintext position i.

§1.3 · Does a cipher symbol tell you anything about its plaintext letter?

Entropy H(X) measures, in bits, how uncertain you are about X: the average number of yes/no questions needed to guess it. Conditional entropy H(X | Y) is your remaining uncertainty once you know Y. If knowing Y helps, H(X | Y) is smaller than H(X). If Y is useless, the two are equal. You estimate both by counting: 

H(P)     = - Σ_p  P(p) log₂ P(p)
H(P | C) = H(P, C) - H(C)          # computed from pair counts (c, p)

Over 172 million aligned character positions:

What you are allowed to look atRemaining uncertainty about the plaintext letter
nothing4.126 bits
the cipher symbol at the same position4.126 bits
that symbol and the previous cipher symbol4.04 bits (the small drop is counting noise from 3,969 contexts)
the position and the cipher symbol3.99 bits (same counting noise, 2,235 contexts)
difference of consecutive cipher symbols (an autokey test)4.123 bits

Knowing the cipher symbol changes nothing. On top of that, the plaintext history appears about 6,000 times in the training set, and every copy gets a different ciphertext. Together these say the cipher behaves like a one-time pad: every sample is encrypted with its own random key. A one-time pad is provably unbreakable. For any ciphertext, every plaintext of the same length is equally likely, so no model, however large, can recover the text.

what this means for Part A

The only thing the ciphertext reveals is the length of the plaintext. The best a model can do is guess the most common character at each position. That is why so many people reported poor accuracy on Moodle: the task is not solvable, so model quality hardly matters. The sensible response is to build each model correctly and simply, train it properly, and explain why the numbers come out as they do. Chasing accuracy with tricks gains nothing.

§1.4 · The resulting task: one label per character

Since cipher position i maps to plaintext position i, decryption becomes sequence labelling: read the cleaned cipher string and output one of 27 classes (space, a–z) at every position. This is much simpler than an encoder–decoder: there is no generation loop and no cross-attention, and the output always has the right length. Each model is just embedding → stack of layers → linear layer to 27 classes, trained with cross-entropy at every position.

§2Why look beyond Transformers at all

Self-attention compares every token with every other token. For a sequence of L tokens that is an L×L score matrix: time and memory grow as L². An RNN instead carries a fixed-size state and updates it once per token, so cost grows as L, and producing the next token costs the same however long the history is. The catch is that a fixed-size state has to forget things. Mamba and DeltaNet are two answers to the question "how do we get the speed of an RNN while forgetting only what doesn't matter?"

TransformerMambaDeltaNet

§3State space models, from zero to Mamba

§3.1 · A continuous-time state space model

A state space model (SSM) comes from control theory. An input signal x(t) drives a hidden state h(t) of size N, and the output y(t) reads from that state: 

h'(t) = A h(t) + B x(t)        # how the state changes over time
y(t)  = C h(t)                 # what we read out

A (N×N) says how the state evolves on its own. If A is negative, the state decays toward zero, which means the model forgets. B (N×1) says how the input is written into the state, and C (1×N) how the state is read out. One SSM handles one scalar input channel. A model with D channels runs D of them side by side.

§3.2 · Discretisation: from a differential equation to a loop

Text is a sequence of steps, not a continuous signal, so we pick a step size Δ and ask what happens to h over one step. With the zero-order hold rule (keep x constant over the step), the exact answer is: 

Ā = exp(Δ A)
B̄ = (Δ A)⁻¹ (exp(Δ A) − I) · Δ B   ≈  Δ B     (Mamba uses this simpler form for B)

h_t = Ā h_{t−1} + B̄ x_t
y_t = C h_t

That is an RNN with no nonlinearity. Δ acts as a knob for how much to remember. With a small Δ, Ā ≈ 1 and B̄ ≈ 0: the state is kept and the input is mostly ignored. With a large Δ, Ā ≈ 0: the old state is wiped and replaced by the current input.

§3.3 · S4: make A diagonal and the loop becomes a convolution

S4 (Gu et al.) made SSMs practical. Two ideas matter here. First, if Ā, B̄, C are the same at every step, you can unroll the loop: y_t = Σ_k C Āk B̄ x_{t−k}. That is a convolution with a kernel K = (CB̄, CĀB̄, CĀ²B̄, …), so training can use one big parallel convolution instead of a loop. Second, A is given a special structure. In the simplified variants Mamba builds on, A is diagonal, so each of the N state dimensions decays at its own rate and exp(ΔA) is just elementwise exp. Mamba initialises A_n = −n for n = 1…N (the "S4D-real" initialisation), giving N different time scales.

§3.4 · Mamba: make Δ, B and C depend on the input

The weakness of S4 is that Ā, B̄, C are the same for every token, so the model cannot decide based on the content "this token matters, store it" or "this token is noise, skip it". Mamba's change, called selectivity, is to compute Δ, B and C from the current token: 

Δ_t = softplus(Linear(x_t))      # (D,)   one step size per channel, always > 0
B_t = Linear(x_t)                # (N,)   what to write
C_t = Linear(x_t)                # (N,)   what to read
h_t = exp(Δ_t A) ⊙ h_{t−1} + Δ_t B_t x_t     # elementwise, per channel
y_t = C_t · h_t + D x_t                      # D is a learned skip connection

The price is that the convolution trick no longer works, since the kernel changes at every step. The Mamba paper fixes this with a hardware-aware parallel scan written in CUDA. Our code instead runs the loop over time in plain PyTorch. It computes exactly the same numbers, only more slowly, and you can read it line by line.

§3.5 · The Mamba block around the SSM

x ─ RMSNorm ─ Linear(D → 2E) ─┬─ u ─ causal depthwise conv(4) ─ SiLU ─ selective SSM ─┐
                              └─ z ─────────────────────────────────── SiLU ───────── ⊙ ─ Linear(E → D) ─ + x

The input is projected up to E = 2D channels and split into two halves. The u half goes through a short convolution of width 4, which mixes each position with its 3 predecessors. This gives cheap local context before the SSM. The z half becomes a gate: after SiLU it is multiplied elementwise with the SSM output, so the block can switch channels off. A Mamba block has no separate MLP, and the gated up/down projection plays that role. That is why the paper stacks two Mamba blocks for every Transformer layer when matching parameter counts. We do the same: 8 Mamba blocks against 4 Transformer layers.

§4Linear attention and the delta rule

§4.1 · Linear attention is an RNN with a matrix state

Softmax attention computes o_t = Σ_{s≤t} softmax(q_t·k_s) v_s. Drop the softmax and the sum can be regrouped: 

o_t = Σ_{s≤t} (q_t · k_s) v_s  =  ( Σ_{s≤t} v_s k_sᵀ ) q_t  =  S_t q_t
S_t = S_{t−1} + v_t k_tᵀ             # a d×d matrix, updated once per token

S is an associative memory: writing v kᵀ stores the pair (k → v), and multiplying by a query reads back a weighted mix of stored values. The problem is that S only ever adds. A d×d matrix can hold about d independent pairs. After that, writes pile on top of each other and reads return a blur of everything.

§4.2 · The delta rule: read, compare, correct

DeltaNet (Schlag et al.; parallelised by Yang et al., 2024) writes into S the way the Widrow–Hoff learning rule does. It first reads what the memory currently returns for key k_t, then writes only the difference between the new value and the old one: 

v_old = S_{t−1} k_t                              # what the memory says now
S_t   = S_{t−1} + β_t (v_t − v_old) k_tᵀ         # move toward the new value
o_t   = S_t q_t

β_t ∈ (0, 1) is a per-token write strength, computed as sigmoid(Linear(x_t)). With β = 1 and a unit-length k, the value stored under k is replaced completely: S_t k_t = v_t exactly. That is why the keys are L2-normalised. Rearranged, the update is S_t = S_{t−1}(I − β k kᵀ) + β v kᵀ: first erase the direction k, then write. In optimisation terms it is one step of gradient descent on the loss ½‖S k_t − v_t‖² with learning rate β_t. The memory is trained online, one token at a time, to map keys to values.

§4.3 · The DeltaNet layer we build

  • q, k, v from one linear projection, then a short causal depthwise convolution (width 4) and SiLU, as in the paper.
  • Split into 4 heads of 64 dimensions. Each head has its own 64×64 memory S.
  • q and k are L2-normalised, and β = sigmoid(W x) gives one value per head per token.
  • The delta-rule recurrence runs over time, followed by an RMSNorm per head and an output projection.
  • As in the paper, a DeltaNet layer replaces attention inside a normal block, so an MLP follows it.

The paper's main contribution is a chunkwise parallel training algorithm (the WY representation) that makes this fast on GPUs. We use the plain recurrent form instead. It gives the same outputs, and every line of it can be explained.

§5The hybrid and its hypothesis

embed → [ Mamba → DeltaNet+MLP ] × 3 → RMSNorm → Linear(27)        ≈ 3.7M parameters

The reasoning in docs/DESIGN.md, which was pushed before any hybrid code was written:

  1. Mamba is a local feature extractor. Its convolution and selective Δ summarise the last few symbols.
  2. DeltaNet is an associative memory. It stores and overwrites key → value pairs, and works best when the keys are already informative.
  3. So Mamba goes first in each pair, and DeltaNet's keys are built from contextual features rather than raw single symbols.
  4. The two types are interleaved so that each memory read can be refined by the next Mamba layer, the way Jamba, Samba and Gated-DeltaNet hybrids interleave their layer types.

H1: at matched size, the hybrid does at least as well as the better pure model. H2: if the cipher really is a one-time pad (§1), all four models will reach the same prior-level accuracy. H1 then cannot be tested on accuracy and the comparison has to rest on loss curves, parameters, FLOPs and speed.

§6.2 · transformer.py

class MLP:        x + down(gelu(up(norm(x))))                 # (B,L,D) → (B,L,4D) → (B,L,D)
class Attention:
    q, k, v = self.qkv(self.norm(x)).chunk(3, dim=-1)        # each (B, L, D)
    q, k, v = [t.view(b, l, H, -1).transpose(1, 2) ...]      # each (B, H, L, d)
    scores = q @ k.transpose(-1, -2) / sqrt(d)               # (B, H, L, L)
    mask = ones(l, l).triu(1)                                # True above the diagonal = the future
    scores = scores.masked_fill(mask, -inf)                  # exp(-inf) = 0 after softmax
    o = scores.softmax(-1) @ v                               # (B, H, L, d)
    return x + self.out(o.transpose(1, 2).reshape(b, l, d))  # heads glued back: (B, L, D)
class TransformerBlock:  mlp(attn(x))                        # both sub-layers add their own residual

Every sub-layer is pre-norm: RMSNorm first, then the operation, then add the input back. The triu(1) mask makes attention causal, so position t only sees positions ≤ t, just like Mamba and DeltaNet, which keeps the comparison fair.

§6.5 · hybrid.py and model.py

def hybrid_blocks(d_model, n_heads, n_pairs):
    blocks = []
    for _ in range(n_pairs):                       # 3 pairs
        blocks.append(MambaBlock(d_model))         # local, selective
        blocks.append(DeltaNetBlock(d_model, n_heads))   # associative memory + MLP
    return blocks

The hybrid is only a list of existing blocks in the order DESIGN.md fixed. It lives in its own file, written after DESIGN.md was pushed. model.py wraps any list of blocks the same way: 

h = embed(x)                                   # (B, L) ints → (B, L, D)
if pos: h = h + pos(arange(L))                 # Transformer only: learned positions
for block in blocks: h = block(h)              # (B, L, D) all the way
return head(norm(h))                           # (B, L, 27) logits, one score per class per position

Mamba and DeltaNet need no positional embedding: they read the sequence in order, so position is built into the recurrence. Attention is order-blind, so the Transformer needs one. build_model(cfg) just picks the block list from the YAML arch field.

§6.6 · train.py

  1. Reads the YAML config, loads .env (W&B key), and seeds torch.
  2. Builds the train set (first 40k samples) and the full validation set, each with a DataLoader.
  3. AdamW (lr 1e-3, weight decay 0.01) with OneCycleLR: a 5% linear warm-up, then cosine decay to almost 0.
  4. Each step: logits (B,L,27) → .transpose(1,2) to (B,27,L), because cross_entropy wants the class axis second → loss averaged over non-padding positions → backward → clip gradient norm at 1 → step.
  5. Every 500 steps: validation loss and per-character accuracy (padding masked out). If accuracy improved, save model.pt.
  6. With --push, upload models/part1/<arch>/ (weights + config) to Arihant25/anlp-a3-<arch>.

§6.7 · decrypt.py and metrics.py

  • load_model: by default calls snapshot_download, which fetches the whole HF repo (model.pt and config.yaml) and returns its local folder (the TAs' setting). --local reads models/part1/ instead.
  • decrypt: encodes each text, splits it into 256-character chunks, and runs all chunks in batches of 64. Takes argmax over the 27 classes, keeps only the real (non-pad) positions, and appends each chunk's prediction back onto its row. The output length always equals the number of non-null cipher characters.
  • count_flops: FlopCounterMode records every matmul and conv in one forward pass of 256 characters, stored as "flops".
  • Metrics: character accuracy compares position by position. Word accuracy does the same on whitespace-split words. Precision / recall / F1 use word overlap (Counter(pred) & Counter(ref), the SQuAD-style bag-of-words match), because position-wise precision would equal accuracy when the lengths match. Levenshtein is the minimum number of single-character inserts, deletes and substitutions turning the prediction into the reference.

§7.2 · What the models actually output

reference:    "robert boulter"
transformer:  "the e         "
mamba:        "the           "
deltanet:     "tee e         "

About 98% of every model's output is the space character, with a few e, t and h, as in the first test text above. Three numbers show that this is the best a model can do here:

  • The loss equals the entropy of the plaintext. Every model's validation loss settles at 2.860 nats per character. Divided by ln 2, that is 4.126 bits, the same H(P) measured in §1.3 before any model was built. Cross-entropy can only go below H(P) if the input says something about the output. It never does, so the models have learned how often each letter occurs and little else.
  • The accuracy equals the frequency of the space. The space is the most common character, 16.87% of the test set. A program that prints only spaces scores 16.87%. The models score 16.79–16.81%, a touch lower because they sometimes guess a letter.
  • The curves are flat from the first evaluation. Validation accuracy is 0.166 at step 500 and 0.166 at step 2,500 for all four models. More data or more steps would not move it.

§7.3 · Reading the other metrics

  • Word accuracy is about 0.2%. A word counts only if the whole word at that position is right, and the models output almost no words.
  • Precision is far higher than recall. Since most of the output is spaces, the models produce very few words. The few they do produce are common ones that often appear somewhere in the reference, so precision is 0.2–0.65. Recall is the share of reference words that were produced, and that is 1–2.5%.
  • Normalised Levenshtein is 0.83. To turn a prediction into its reference you would have to change 83% of the characters. The other 17% are the spaces that happened to line up.
  • The differences between models in these columns are noise. They come down to which few letters a model happens to guess, such as "the" or "tee".

§7.4 · The hypothesis, and what can still be compared

H2 held: all four models reach the same prior-level accuracy, as predicted for a one-time pad. That means H1 cannot be tested on accuracy. The hybrid ties with the pure models because every model ties. What is left to compare is cost:

  • Parameters and FLOPs are close by design. The models have 3.2–3.7M parameters and need 1.7–1.9 GFLOPs for 256 characters. The hybrid is the largest on both, since each pair holds a Mamba block and a full DeltaNet block with its MLP.
  • Wall-clock time is very different. The Transformer trains in 1.5 minutes. Mamba takes 149, DeltaNet 73 and the hybrid 116. The hybrid lands between the two pure models because it is made of three blocks of each.
FLOPs and seconds measure different things

Mamba needs fewer FLOPs than the Transformer and still trains about 100 times slower. Attention is a few large matrix products that the GPU runs in parallel. Our scans are Python loops of 256 steps, and each step sends several tiny operations to the GPU, which sits about 90% idle waiting for the next one. The slowness comes from the simple loop, and the architectures themselves are fast: the Mamba paper uses a parallel scan written in CUDA, and the DeltaNet paper a chunkwise algorithm. We kept the loop so that every line can be explained.

where the weights are

The four checkpoints are public on Hugging Face as Arihant25/anlp-a3-transformer, -mamba, -deltanet and -hybrid. bash scripts/part1/test_assn.sh --heldout uses the Transformer, which has the highest validation accuracy by a hair and is by far the fastest.

§8Part B concepts: news as a graph

§8.1 · The UPFD GossipCop dataset

Each sample is one news article and everyone who retweeted it. Node 0 is the article. Every other node is a Twitter user, and an edge u → v means the story reached v through u. The label says whether the article is fake. GossipCop (celebrity news) has 5,464 graphs, split by the dataset itself into 20% train, 10% validation and 70% test, and we must not re-split. Each node comes with one of four feature vectors:

featuresizewhat it encodes
profile10account metadata: followers, friends, status count, account age, verified, …
spacy300average word2vec-style spaCy vectors of the user's recent tweets (and of the article for node 0)
bert768BERT sentence embedding of the same text
content310spacy (300) + profile (10) concatenated

§8.2 · Message passing, the one idea behind every GNN here

A graph neural network layer updates every node by gathering its neighbours' vectors, combining them, and mixing the result with the node's own vector. Stack two layers and each node sees neighbours two hops away. To classify a whole graph, pool all node vectors into one (we take the mean) and apply a linear layer. In code, "gather from neighbours" is a single operation. The edge list edge_index is a (2, E) tensor of (source, destination) pairs, and 

out = zeros(n, d).index_add_(0, dst, messages)     # add each edge's message into its destination row

sums the messages arriving at each node. Each model below differs only in what the message is and how it is weighted.

§8.3 · The five models

modelupdate for node iidea
Root-only MLPMLP(x_0)ignore the graph, read only the article. This is the text-only baseline.
GCN (Kipf & Welling)Σ_{j∈N(i)∪{i}} W x_j / √(d_i d_j)average over neighbours and self. The 1/√(d_i d_j) factor stops high-degree nodes from blowing up.
GraphSAGE (Hamilton et al.)W₁ x_i + W₂ · mean_{j∈N(i)} x_jkeep self and neighbours in separate weights, so a node is not drowned out by its neighbours.
GAT (Veličković et al.)Σ_j α_ij W x_j, with α = softmax_j LeakyReLU(aᵀ[Wx_i ‖ Wx_j])learn how much each neighbour matters. Attention is computed only over edges, with 4 heads.
Bi-GCN (Bian et al.)a GCN on the edges + a GCN on the reversed edges, with root features concatenated after each layera story spreads top-down (source → retweeters) and responses travel bottom-up. Model both, and keep reminding each node what the source said.
which Bi-GCN?

The PDF links arXiv 2101.05519, which is the Binary GCN paper (1-bit weights for efficiency). The model usually called Bi-GCN for propagation trees is Bian et al. 2020 (bi-directional). A student asked on Moodle which was meant and got no reply. We implement Bian et al., because it is the one designed for propagation graphs, and record that choice in CHOICES.md.

§8.4 · Evaluation protocol

  • For each run, train for 60 epochs and keep the weights from the epoch with the best validation macro-F1. Evaluate those on test.
  • Repeat with seeds 0–4 and report mean ± standard deviation.
  • Macro precision, recall and F1 compute the metric for the "fake" class and the "real" class separately and then average the two. Since the classes are balanced, this treats both kinds of mistake equally.
  • The ablation fixes GraphSAGE and every hyperparameter, and changes only the node features.

§9Part B code, line by line

PyG's DataLoader merges a batch of graphs into one big disconnected graph. data.x is (n, F), the features of every node in the batch. data.edge_index is (2, E), with node ids already shifted so they point into that big graph. data.batch is (n,) and says which graph each node belongs to. data.ptr is (G+1,), where each graph's first node starts, so data.ptr[:-1] gives the index of every root (node 0).

§9.1 · Helpers

FunctionWhat it does
add_self_loops(edge_index, n)appends the edges (i, i) for every node, so a node's own vector is part of its update
undirected(edge_index)adds every edge in reverse too, so information flows both ways
scatter_sum(values, index, n)zeros(n, ...).index_add_(0, index, values): row index[e] receives values[e]. This is the "gather from neighbours" step.
mean_pool(h, batch, G)scatter-sum node vectors by graph id and divide by the node count from bincount(batch): (n, D) → (G, D)
torch.bincount(idx, minlength=n)counts how many times each number 0…n−1 appears in idx. With dst, that is each node's in-degree.

§9.2 · GCNLayer

src, dst = add_self_loops(edge_index, n)
deg  = torch.bincount(dst, minlength=n)              # in-degree of each node (incl. itself)
norm = deg[src].rsqrt() * deg[dst].rsqrt()           # 1/√(d_j d_i) per edge
h    = self.lin(x)                                   # W x, no bias yet
return scatter_sum(norm[:, None] * h[src], dst, n) + self.bias

h[src] picks the transformed sender vector for every edge (E, D), scales it by the edge's normalisation, and adds it into the receiver's row. The bias is added after aggregation, as in the paper. If it were inside lin, it would be summed once per neighbour.

§9.3 · SAGELayer

neigh = scatter_sum(x[src], dst, n) / deg[:, None]   # mean of neighbours (deg clamped ≥ 1)
return self.lin_self(x) + self.lin_neigh(neigh)

Two separate weight matrices: one for the node itself, one for its neighbourhood average. There are no self-loops.

§9.4 · GATLayer

h = self.lin(x).view(n, heads, -1)                                   # (n, 4, 32)
e = leaky_relu((h*att_src).sum(-1)[src] + (h*att_dst).sum(-1)[dst])  # (E, 4) score per edge per head
node_max = zeros(n, 4).index_reduce_(0, dst, e, "amax", include_self=False)   # each node's largest score
w = (e - node_max[dst]).exp()                                        # stable exp, at most 1
alpha = w / scatter_sum(w, dst, n)[dst]                              # softmax over each node's incoming edges
return scatter_sum(alpha[:, :, None] * h[src], dst, n).reshape(n, -1) + self.bias

The paper's score aᵀ[Wh_i ‖ Wh_j] splits into a_dstᵀWh_i + a_srcᵀWh_j, which is why there are two attention vectors. The softmax groups edges by receiver: scatter_sum(w, dst) is each node's denominator, and [dst] copies it back to every edge. index_reduce_(..., "amax") is the max-version of index_add_: it finds each receiver's largest score. Subtracting it does not change the softmax (every score in a group shifts by the same constant). It stops exp from overflowing, and each node's best edge gets weight exp(0) = 1, so the denominator can never underflow to 0.

ClassForward

§9.6 · train.py

  • run(model, feature, seed): seed torch, load the three fixed UPFD splits, Adam (lr 1e-3, weight decay 0.01), 60 epochs. After every epoch, log the mean training loss, compute validation metrics and deep-copy the weights if validation macro-F1 improved. At the end, load the best weights and evaluate on test.
  • evaluate: argmax predictions, then sklearn accuracy_score and precision_recall_fscore_support(average="macro").
  • main: experiment part_a = 5 models × BERT, part_b = GraphSAGE × 4 features, each × 5 seeds. Writes the means in the template's layout to output/part2/results.json (or results_ta.json with --ta), and every run plus mean and std to results/part2/.
  • W&B logs every run when a key is present. Otherwise W&B is disabled, so the TAs' run does not need an account.

§11Viva questions, answered out loud

Short answers you can say without notes. Each one points back to the section with the details.

  1. Why are your Part A accuracies so low?

    "Because the cipher can't be broken. Once the punctuation nulls are removed, every cipher symbol lines up with one plaintext letter, but the cipher symbol carries zero information about that letter. Over 172 million positions, H(plain | cipher) equals H(plain), 4.126 bits. The same word gets a different ciphertext in every sample. That's a one-time pad. The only thing leaked is the length, so the best possible model predicts the most common character at each position, which is about 17% character accuracy. All four of my models converge there, and their loss equals the plaintext entropy." (§1, §7)

  2. Why drop the punctuation symbols?

    "After deleting them, the ciphertext length equals the plaintext length in 100% of training samples, and their positions are independent of word boundaries: they sit next to a space 16.97% of the time against a base rate of 16.95%. So they're nulls. Dropping them gives a 1:1 alignment, which turns decryption into per-character classification, with no decoder and no length problem." (§1.2)

  3. Write the Mamba recurrence and explain Δ.

    "h_t = exp(Δ_t A) h_{t−1} + Δ_t B_t x_t, y_t = C_t h_t + D x_t. A is diagonal and negative, initialised to −1…−16. Δ_t = softplus(Linear(x_t)) is the step size. A small Δ keeps the state and ignores the input, and a large Δ resets the state to the current input. Because Δ, B and C come from the input, the model chooses per token what to remember. That's selectivity, and it's the difference from S4." (§3.4)

  4. Why is A stored as A_log?

    "A = −exp(A_log) is always negative whatever value the optimiser gives A_log, so exp(ΔA) is always below 1 and the state can't blow up."

  5. What does the conv in a Mamba block do, and why is it causal?

    "It's a depthwise width-4 convolution: each channel mixes itself with its previous 3 positions. That gives cheap local context before the SSM. I pad by 3 on both sides and keep only the first L outputs, so output t depends only on inputs t−3 to t."

  6. Write the delta rule and say why it beats linear attention.

    "S_t = S_{t−1} + β_t (v_t − S_{t−1} k_t) k_tᵀ, o_t = S_t q_t. Linear attention only adds v kᵀ, so old associations pile up and reads get blurry once more pairs are stored than there are dimensions. The delta rule first reads what's stored under k, then writes only the difference. With unit k and β = 1, the old value is replaced exactly. It's one gradient step on ‖S k − v‖²." (§4.2)

  7. Why L2-normalise q and k in DeltaNet?

    "With ‖k‖ = 1, the matrix I − β k kᵀ has eigenvalue 1 − β along k and 1 everywhere else. For β in (0, 1) it never stretches the memory, so S stays bounded, and β = 1 would mean 'replace exactly'. Without normalisation, a large key could make the update overshoot and diverge."

  8. Why is your Mamba 8 layers but the others 4?

    "A Mamba block has no MLP. Its gated expand-and-project plays that role, so one block has about half the parameters of a Transformer layer. The Mamba paper matches sizes by using two Mamba blocks per Transformer layer. With 8 blocks, all models land between 3.2M and 3.7M parameters."

  9. Explain your hybrid and whether the hypothesis held.

    "Three pairs of Mamba followed by DeltaNet+MLP. Mamba builds local context, and DeltaNet uses it to form keys for its associative memory. Interleaving lets each read be refined by the next Mamba layer. H1 said it would match or beat both pure models. H2 said that if the cipher is a one-time pad, all models tie at the prior. The results match H2, so accuracy can't test H1. What the results do show is the hybrid's cost relative to the pure models." (§5, §7)

  10. How did you count FLOPs, and what does it miss?

    "torch.utils.flop_counter.FlopCounterMode on one forward pass of 256 characters. It counts matmuls and convolutions, at 2 FLOPs per multiply-add. Mamba's scan is elementwise, so it isn't counted. DeltaNet's state update is an einsum, so it is. Attention's L² cost shows up in the two batched matmuls."

  11. Why per-chunk inference instead of the full sequence?

    "Training saw at most 256 characters, and the Transformer's learned positions only go up to 256. Splitting the test texts the same way keeps train and test identical and caps memory, even for 24k-character inputs. Here it costs nothing, because there is no information that crosses positions anyway."

  12. Does the propagation graph help? Which model is best?

    "Yes, by a lot: about 74% F1 from the article alone against 93–95% with the graph. GAT and GCN are tied at 94.8, Bi-GCN is at 94.4 and GraphSAGE at 92.8. The graphs are shallow stars (77% of users at depth 1), so two layers of any aggregator see almost everything." (§10.1)

  13. Which features matter most?

    "Content, which is spaCy + profile, at 94.9. Then BERT at 92.9, spaCy at 91.5 and profile at 86.0. User metadata alone is surprisingly strong, and combining it with text beats a bigger text encoder, so the two are complementary." (§10.2)

  14. Write GCN's update and explain the normalisation.

    "h_i' = Σ_{j ∈ N(i) ∪ {i}} W h_j / √(d_i d_j). Without it, a node's output would scale with its degree. The symmetric square-root form comes from normalising the adjacency matrix with self-loops, D^{−1/2}(A+I)D^{−1/2}. In code: add self-loops, count in-degrees with bincount, weight each edge, and index_add_ the weighted messages into their destinations."

  15. Why macro-F1?

    "It's the average of the F1 for 'fake' and the F1 for 'real', so neither class dominates. GossipCop is balanced (49% fake), so macro and binary F1 come out close, but macro doesn't depend on which class we call positive."
